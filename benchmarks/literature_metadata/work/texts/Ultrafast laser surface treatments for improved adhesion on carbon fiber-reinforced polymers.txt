This study investigated the influence of the wavelength, pulse duration, fluence, and overscan number laser parameters on CFRP surface treatment and bonding per­ formance. The extent of the resin residue and fiber damage on the CFRP surfaces following laser treatment was assessed utilizing image analysis software. The surface morphology of the CFRP was scrutinized using laser confocal microscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Contact angle measurements and X-ray photo­ electron spectroscopy (XPS) were used to evaluate the surface wettability and chemical composition of the CFRP. The adhesive joint capabilities were rigorously tested to determine the causes of failures. The results revealed that laser treatment, which removed resin and integrated oxygen-containing functional groups, substantially increased the joint bonding strength. Notably, the femtosecond and picosecond green lasers at a fluence of 5.18 J/cm2 significantly increased the shear strengths by 398.9 % and 393.9 %. A picosecond infrared laser at 3.69 J/ cm2 and a femtosecond infrared laser at 2.23 J/cm2 achieved shear strength enhancements of 324.3 % and 337.9 %. Pre-treatment with a femtosecond green laser yielded the optimal shear strength. This study demonstrates that appropriate laser surface treatment can enhance CFRP bonding performance, particularly with the femto­ second lasers exhibiting superior shear strength enhancement. These findings offer a new strategy for efficient CFRP bonding and set the stage for further research to optimize laser parameters for broader industrial applications. 1. Introduction Carbon fiber-reinforced polymers (CFRPs) are extensively used in the aerospace industry for critical structural components such as fuselages, wings, and skins [1]. These materials account for over 50 % of the total weight of an aircraft. Compared to traditional metals, CFRP can signif­ icantly reduce weight and enhance strength, corrosion resistance, and thermal stability. Mechanical connection methods such as fastening and riveting are commonly employed in the aviation industry [2]. Typically, these methods require drilling holes into a substrate, which can damage the substrate and degrade its mechanical properties due to stress con­ centration from drilling. Adhesive bonding is preferred for the joining of composite materials because it prevents substrate damage. Before bonding, mandatory surface pre-treatment is required to eliminate contaminants such as dirt, microorganisms, and mold release agents, improving surface wettability and chemical reactivity [3]. Surface treatments include mechanical methods such as sandblasting, grinding, stripping, and milling. Alternatively, chemical treatments and laser surface treatments can be applied [4]. In mechanical treatment, CFRP fibers may sustain significant damage. The chemical treatment of CFRP may result in an uncontrolled surface and produce waste. Laser surface treatment, which is a non-contact method, offers precision, custom­ izability, and minimal contamination, thus achieving a uniform surface with minimal damage. The effectiveness of CFRP laser surface treatment has already been established. Laser scanning removes the top resin layer, revealing the underlying fiber layer and significantly enhancing the shear strength of CFRP after bonding [5]. The predominant techniques for CFRP surface treatment included CO2 continuous lasers and UV nano­ second lasers [6–10]. Regarding CFRP’s capacity to absorb various wavelengths, a far-infrared laser at a 10.6 μm wavelength [11] and UV lasers within the 200–320 nm spectrum exhibit a resin absorption rate of 90 % [7]. This high absorption rate makes these lasers suitable for the removal of the surface resin layer in CFRP. However, continuous CO2 lasers can cause thermal damage to CFRP, resulting in fiber delamina­ tion and extrusion. Ultraviolet nanosecond lasers have a lower thermal impact. However, the low energy output reduces the processing effi­ ciency and results in higher equipment costs, making it less economi­ cally viable. Therefore, research has increasingly focused on 1.06 μm infrared lasers [5,12–17]. At a wavelength of 1.06 μm, an infrared laser penetrates the resin layer, directly heating the underlying carbon fibers. Once the resin reaches its melting or vaporization point, fiber removal is facilitated through the melting or vaporization of the resin [5]. Previous studies evaluated how infrared nanosecond, picosecond, and femtosecond lasers with different pulse durations affected CFRP surface treatment, confirming the suitability of near-infrared lasers. The infrared nanosecond laser, which is marked by its prolonged pulse duration and increased thermal effect, poses a risk of fiber damage, making it challenging to determine optimal processing parameters [5]. Oliveira et al. employed an infrared femtosecond laser with a pulse duration of 550 fs to selectively remove resin from a CFRP surface [18,19]. This resulted in the formation of laser-induced periodic surface structures (LIPSS) on the fibers. However, no further mechanical prop­ erty tests were reported. Li et al. conducted a study to assess the effects of infrared picosecond laser treatment compared to UV nanosecond treatment [20]. The results indicated superior adhesive joint quality and higher shear strength with infrared picosecond laser treatment. However, a limitation was the absence of further optimization of the process parameters. Additionally, no experimental research on green laser treatment for CFRP surfaces has yet been reported. Further investigation is needed on the applications of infrared picosecond and femtosecond lasers, as well as the mechanical property assessment of bonded surfaces. This study employed four distinct laser configurations with unique wavelengths and pulse dura­ tions: Green femtosecond (GR-fs) lasers, green picosecond (GR-ps) la­ sers, infrared femtosecond (IR-fs) lasers, and infrared picosecond (IR-ps) lasers. These four different laser setups were used to assess the effects of these treatments on CFRP surface morphology and interfacial bonding. The surface morphology of CFRP was analyzed using an SEM, and ImageJ software was utilized to characterize the surface condition and calculate parameters including the resin removal rate and the fiber damage rate. The interfacial bond between CFRP materials exhibited high fracture resistance. The bond’s shear strength was determined by the interfacial strength between the adhesive layer and the CFRP, in conjunction with the post-treatment adhesive layer’s strength at the bonded interface. Following surface treatment, the interfacial strength at the adhesive- bonded interface was ascertained by calculating the product of the bond strength and the contact area per unit of area. The surface roughness and wettability were assessed after laser processing to determine the adhesive spreading efficacy on the CFRP surface. These factors significantly affected the contact area between the material and the colloid. The surface underwent XPS analysis to evaluate changes in the chemical bond strength that were attributable to its chemical composition. Ultimately, the bond integrity of the CFRP after curing was assessed, with the mechanical properties being evaluated through rigorous testing and assessment. 2. Experimental procedures 2.1. Lasers and materials An ultrafast laser (FemtoYL-Vary, Wuhan Yangtze Soton Laser, Ltd.) with an adjustable pulse duration and an adjustable repetition rate was employed. The detailed laser parameters are provided in Table 2. The laser beam was directed by a mirror and expanded from 3 mm to 9 mm by a beam expander before entering a galvanometer. The pulse energy was adjusted with a half-wave plate and a polarization beam splitter (PBS). The beam was subsequently focused by an f-theta lens onto the sample surface, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). Crosswise laser scanning, as shown in Fig. 1(b), was utilized due to the bi-directional weaving of the fibers [21]. T700 carbon fiber composite, which was produced by Toray of Japan and prepared according to GB/T33334-2016, with dimensions of 100 mm × 25 mm × 2 mm, was utilized in the experiments. The matrix material was epoxy resin, with carbon fibers comprising a volume content ranging from 60 % to 65 %. Each ply featured a fiber direction of 90◦, and the symmetric ply structure prevented the “coupling effect” associated with ply asymmetry. Fig. 2(a) shows that the carbon fiber samples exhibited bi-directional and plain-woven fibers on their surface. Fig. 2(b) shows the initial resin layer with nonuniform thickness. The thickness of this layer varied from a maximum of 153.72 ± 10 μm to a minimum of 30 ± 10 μm. Additionally, the fiber layer thickness was 139.45 ± 10 μm. 2.2. The resin removal rate and the fiber damage rate on the surface of the CFRP were quantified using ImageJ software for the microscopic surface morphology images. Fig. 4 illustrates the method for quantifying the resin removal rate using ImageJ software. Microscope images were uploaded to the ImageJ software and processed into a binary format at a consistent threshold range. The “wand tool” was used to identify the resin removal area, and the pixel count within this area was calculated using the Analyze-Measure function in the ImageJ software. This rate was determined to be the ratio of the removed resin pixels to the total pixels. Fig. 5 illustrates the fiber damage rate calculation process in ImageJ. The original microscope images were imported into ImageJ and converted into binary maps with the same threshold range. Intact fibers were extracted using the filtering function of Analyze Particles and counted using the counting tool in the ImageJ software [22]. Specifically, undamaged carbon fibers from the 1200X microscopic images were initially selected as a reference to ascertain the standard dimensions, measuring the length (L), width (D), and area (S) of the fibers (Fig. 5(d)). These dimensions served as benchmarks for undam­ aged fibers. Subsequently, the same parameters were measured for fi­ bers on the CFRP surface after laser treatment, yielding the length (l), width (d), and area (s) of the treated fibers (Fig. 5(e)). A comparative analysis of the dimensions pre-treatment and post-treatment was per­ formed to identify fibers with any dimension (l, d, or s) falling below the established standard as damaged (Fig. 5(f)). The damage rate was determined by calculating the ratio of the number of damaged fibers to the total number of fibers. This methodology provided an accurate quantitative approach to assess the impact of the laser treatment on the integrity of fibers on the CFRP surface, offering a scientific basis for process optimization and material performance evaluation. 3. Results and discussion 3.1. Surface topography analysis The microstructure of the CFRP revealed that the various laser treatments yielded distinct outcomes. After treatment with GR-fs and GR-ps, the remaining resin in the outer layer firmly adhered to the fibers, as shown in Fig. 6(a) and (b). Furthermore, the surface of the resin exhibited various indentations and protrusions that resulted from the interaction between the green laser and the resin. The resin underwent gradual thermal decomposition and peeling during each laser scan, which mirrored the resin removal mechanism observed with UV and 10.6 μm wavelength CO2 continuous lasers. After IR-fs and IR-ps treat­ ments, as shown in Fig. 6(c) and (d), a notable separation appeared between the resin layer and the fiber layer, with the remaining resin becoming flaky. The resin layer treated with the IR-fs laser exhibited a flat surface with small pits, whereas the resin layer treated with the IR-ps laser revealed minimal ablation and a highly smooth surface. The infrared laser did not interact with the resin. Instead, it penetrated the resin and affected the fiber layer. The energy that was transferred to the fiber layer raised the temperature and caused the temperature to reach the melting point of the resin. This led to the melting of the inner resin layer, which was in direct contact with the fibers. As the temperature rose, the interface between the resin and fibers generated recoil pres­ sure, which resulted in the detachment of the resin [23]. Following the initial removal of the resin by the laser, subsequent scans directly affected each fiber, which resulted in the formation of the LIPSS struc­ ture, as depicted in Fig. 6(a), (b), (c), and (d). This micro-nano structure effectively increased the surface area of the CFRP and enhanced its surface wettability. The LIPSS formed by GR-fs and GR-ps exhibited a spatial period of 417 nm, whereas those generated by IR-fs and IR-ps showed a spatial period of 833 nm. Both types of LIPSS were classified as low-spatial frequency LIPSS (LSFL), with the green laser’s shorter wavelength resulting in a reduced period for the generated LIPSS [19,24]. The cross-scanning technique was employed to modify the orientation of the LIPSS on the fibers. The epoxy adhesive exhibited a low affinity for the surface mold release agent and the surface epoxy resin, resulting in poor binding ability [16]. The use of a laser to eliminate the surface resin and mold release agent could improve the adhesive bonding capacity of the CFRP. However, the fibers exposed after removing the resin were vulnerable to damage. An SEM was employed to evaluate the surface topography of the CFRP. The SEM could not provide a quantitative analysis of the ef­ fects of the resin removal on the surface or the extent of the fiber damage. The image processing software ImageJ could be utilized to analyze and manipulate the surface topography images, as illustrated in Fig. 4 and Fig. 5, to determine the resin removal rate and the fiber damage rate for the CFRP surface. The results illustrated in Fig. 7 (a) showed successful resin removal with all four laser setups, with the infrared femtosecond laser proving to be particularly effective. This laser could remove surface resin with only 20 overscans, utilizing an energy density of 2.22 J/cm2. To obtain a resin removal rate exceeding 85 %, the infrared picosecond, green picosecond, and green femto­ second lasers necessitated an energy density of 3.69 J/cm2. Fig. 7 (b) illustrates the fact that an infrared laser at an equivalent energy density of 3.69 J/cm2 removed the resin more effectively than a green laser. The resin on the surface could be predominantly removed in 10 overscans using the infrared laser, whereas the green laser necessitated 20 over­ scans. Furthermore, a laser source with a shorter pulse duration effec­ tively removed the resin. Therefore, regarding the effectiveness of eliminating resin, the order was as follows: IR-fs > IR-ps > GR-fs > GR- ps. Further excessive scanning might lead to the fracturing and extrac­ tion of the carbon fibers during the adhesive removal from the surface layer. Damage to the carbon fibers reduced the material’s mechanical properties and adversely affected the shear strength of the carbon fiber plate [17]. Surface roughness analysis The increase of the surface roughness of the CFRP expanded the binder and contact surface area, thereby enhancing the interfacial strength at the joint and improving the mechanical interlocking ability [25]. The surface roughness of the initial sample, denoted as Sa, was measured at 18 nm. After laser scanning, the treated sample showed an increase in Sa, which was correlated with both the increase in the laser energy density and the number of overscans. Once the energy density exceeded 5.18 J/cm2, the fiber layer experienced significant damage, causing a reduction in Sa. Furthermore, the excessive number of over­ scans, as illustrated in Fig. 9, also reduced Sa. Therefore, the optimiza­ tion of the process parameters, such as the fluence and the number of overscans, was crucial. 3.3. Contact angle analysis The adhesive’s capacity to wet the surface of the sample during cementation influenced the effective contact area. A smaller contact angle enabled the adhesive to spread more rapidly over the CFRP sur­ face, thereby enhancing the surface wetting. The adhesive that was employed in the shear tests was Henkel Loctite E-120HP epoxy resin AB adhesive, which primarily consisted of epoxy resin. This adhesive exhibited a chemical polarity similar to that of glycerol [26]. Conse­ quently, glycerol was selected to test the surface wettability. The contact angle of the glycerol on the untreated sample was measured to be 86.9◦. The contact angle of the glycerol decreased with the increasing energy density and number of overscans for both GR-fs and GR-ps treatments, as illustrated in Fig. 10.
