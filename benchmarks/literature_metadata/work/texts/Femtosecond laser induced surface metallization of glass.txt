This paper investigates the metallization process of a glass surface using a femtosecond laser, analyzes the mechanism of laser-induced-plasma-assisted ablation (LIPAA) in achieving glass surface metallization. At the same time, some samples were treated with hydrogenation reduction to reduce copper oxide to copper. Furthermore, a systematic parameter study was conducted to investigate the effects of different single pulse energies, scanning speeds, and numbers of scanning layers on the thickness, surface roughness, percentage content of copper and compactness of the metallized layer. The experimental results shows that high-quality metallized layers can be prepared when the scanning speed was 100 mm/s, the single pulse energy was 40 μJ, and the number of scanning layers was 5. In this work, a new preparation approach for the metallization of glass surfaces and a quantitative reference for the preparation process was provided. 1. Introduction The metallization of a glass surface involves using glass as a substrate and metal as a target material to deposit a metal film layer on the glass surface through chemical and physical methods to achieve specific functions [1–5]. Compared to changing the characteristics of the entire glass, surface treatment methods are more widely recognized [6–8]. Metallization of the glass surface is one such method, providing wear resistance, corrosion resistance, sealing properties, electrical conduc­ tivity, and magnetic conductivity [9–14]. These properties make metallized glass surfaces widely used in solar cells [15,16], display screens [17,18], architecture [19,20], and automobiles [21,22], estab­ lishing it as a new research hotspot. For example, metal coatings with silver, aluminum, zinc, or titanium on glass surfaces can improve corrosion resistance, thermal insulation, and sound insulation [23,24], making them suitable for architectural glass. In the field of automobile manufacturing, aluminum is commonly used for surface metallization of glass, which can improve the reflection of infrared and ultraviolet rays, thereby improving the thermal insulation and opacity of the glass [25,26]. In the field of solar cells, glass used for packaging and pro­ tecting solar cells is usually surface-metallized with transparent conductive oxides such as tin oxide (SnO2) or indium tin oxide (ITO) to improve transparency and conductivity, significantly boosting the effi­ ciency of solar cells [27,28]. In recent years, an increasing number of methods have been devel­ oped for the metallization of glass surfaces. Sundberg et al. deposited transparent and amorphous Al2O3 thin films on the glass surface using metal organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) during the thermal strengthening process, and discussed the mechanical properties of the glass surface after metallization [29]. Wang et al. used activated com­ bustion high velocity air fuel (AC-HVAF) spray method to spray nickel based amorphous metal coatings on substrates, and discussed the for­ mation and properties of metal coatings. The substrate can be glass [30]. Zhang et al. deposited nickel on hollow glass microspheres (HGMs) by chemical plating after roughening HGMs using a mixed solution of NaF and HCl and analyzed the uniformity and adhesion of Ni deposition [31]. Markowska Szczupak et al. prepared silver, copper, and gold monolayer and bilayer nano metal films on glass substrates using magnetron sputtering technology, and systematically studied their antibacterial properties [32]. As a new method of glass surface metallization, laser-induced- plasma-assisted ablation (LIPAA) has garnered widespread attention due to its low cost, high processing efficiency, ease of acquisition, and minimal environmental pollution [33–35]. Compared with picosecond and nanosecond lasers, femtosecond lasers offer a smaller heat-affected zone, higher processing accuracy, and higher peak power, resulting in greater energy density. The extremely high peak power and energy density of femtosecond lasers can induce the generation of plasma. Therefore, studying the mechanism of the interaction between glass and copper under femtosecond laser irradiation is of significant importance. Therefore, this work used femtosecond lasers to metallize a glass sur­ face, forming a metallized thin film of copper with a certain thickness on the glass. It also elucidated the LIPAA mechanism under femtosecond laser irradiation using plasma sputtering. Suitable processing parame­ ters were identified by comparing the surface roughness, thickness, compactness, percentage content of copper and bright copper area in the metallized layer on the glass surface under different laser parameters. 2. Experimentation 2.1. Experimental setup and materials The experiment was conducted under standard ambient conditions at a constant temperature of 25 ◦C and atmospheric pressure (~101.3 kPa). The laser used in the experiment is the SPRIT 16 HE SHG femtosecond laser from Newport Corporation in the United States with a wavelength of 1040 nm, a pulse width of 388 fs, a focal spot diameter of 14 μm and a repetition frequency of 100 kHz. The specific metallization process is shown in the Fig. 1. The raw materials used in the experiment include a 7101-glass slide and copper foil, with the glass slide having a thickness of 1 mm and the electrolytic copper foil having a thickness of 60 μm. The processing area of the samples is a 5 mm × 5 mm square. During the experiment, the copper foil was sandwiched between two glass slides. Transparent tape was wrapped around the non-processing area to apply a minimal, consistent pressure, ensuring stable mechani­ cal contact and minimizing air gaps between the layers. This setup guaranteed a consistent environment for the subsequent plasma- mediated deposition process. It is important to note that while pres­ sure is applied here for mechanical stability, its role differs fundamen­ tally from that in Laser Transmission Welding (LTW), where intimate optical contact is crucial for linear energy transfer and interfacial heating [36]. In our approach, the laser beam was focused through the glass onto the surface of the copper foil, and the primary mechanism is nonlinear absorption and plasma sputtering rather than linear absorp­ tion through an optically contacted interface. The sample was then scanned in a row-by-row manner, filling a 5 mm × 5 mm square with parallel lines. A bottom-up processing strategy was employed, with the laser focal position elevated by 20 μm after each layer. To determine the initial focal position accounting for refractive index mismatch, we considered that the glass (nglass ≈1.5) shifts the actual focal spot axially relative to the geometric focus in air (nair ≈1). Theoretically, the focal shift ΔZ can be estimated as ΔZ=(d * nair) / nglass, where d is the intended focusing depth inside the glass. However, in the LIPAA process, the presence of metal plasma and nonlinear absorption effects under femtosecond laser irradiation complicates theoretical predictions, rendering them potentially inaccurate. Therefore, an experimental calibration was performed to identify the effective focal plane for robust LIPAA processing. The surface position (Z = 0) was defined as the height where the brightest plasma was observed when focusing at the glass–air interface (Fig. 1(a)). The focus was then raised incrementally by 20 μm steps followed by test irradia­ tions. The most consistent and effective metallization was achieved not at Z = 0, but at Z = –50 μm (50 μm below the surface). Throughout the process, laser-induced plasma sputtered onto the glass surface, resulting in the formation of a metallized layer. Assuming a conservative effective absorptivity (A = 0.4) for the femtosecond laser irradiation on copper [39,40], the estimated maximum temperature rise at the copper surface is approximately 88 ◦C. This result indicates the interface temperature remains near ambient conditions (~113 ◦C) and is far below the softening point of the boro­ silicate glass substrate (~1200 ◦C). Therefore, thermal melting of the glass is deemed highly improbable. Furthermore, the significant thermal mismatch between copper (high thermal conductivity, ~400 W/m⋅K) and glass (low thermal conductivity, ~1 W/m⋅K) further inhibits heat diffusion into the substrate. This transient thermal condition is consis­ tent with the model established by Li et al. [41] for analyzing substrate temperatures under pulsed laser exposure. As demonstrated by this model and its application in joining dissimilar materials [42], the vast difference in thermal conductivity results in the confinement of heat within the copper foil. Consequently, while localized melting of the copper undoubtedly occurs, the energy deposition at the glass interface is insufficient to bring its temperature close to the softening point, which is corroborated by our experimental observation of a damage-free glass substrate. Second, the potential for direct laser ablation of the glass was assessed. The calculated peak laser fluence (~4.9 J/cm2) is below the single-pulse ablation threshold for fused silica (~10 J/cm2 for compa­ rable pulse parameters [43]). Crucially, the opaque copper film acts as a shield, preventing the glass substrate from being exposed to the full, focused laser intensity. The glass is primarily subjected to scattered light, residual heat, and plasma pressure, none of which are capable of causing direct ablation.Third, the role of the laser-induced copper plasma was examined. While such plasmas can generate transient high pressures (estimated here at ~ 2.5 GPa based on models of previous works [44,45]) and temperatures, two factors prevent significant glass modification: Thermal Transience: The plasma lifetime (nanoseconds to micro­ seconds[46]) is orders of magnitude shorter than the time required for heat to conduct into the glass bulk to create a stable melt pool (τ ≈5 ms for a 100 nm layer). Mechanical Strength: The estimated peak pressure, while high, remains below the reported spallation threshold (>4 GPa [47,48]) for fused silica under nanosecond-scale loading. The glass is expected to deform elastically under this transient stress without fracturing. Conclusion on Debris Origin Based on this analysis, the formation of a copper-glass melt mixture is physically implausible. The material covering the laser processing track is attributed to redeposited ablated copper nanoparticles—a typical phenomenon in confined ablation geometries [49,50]. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the process whereby the high-pressure plasma plume ejectes ablated material onto adjacent cooler surfaces for redeposition is more consistent with the actual experimental observations. First, when the laser pulse reached the surface of the copper foil, the energy of the laser was absorbed by the copper foil, a large amount of thermal energy was generated and thus the temperature of the copper foil was increased. The generated thermal energy diffuses around the processing area and inside the material via thermal conduction. For the lasers, the energy on the spot followed a Gaussian distribution in the radial direction, with the highest energy and thermal output at the center of the spot. When the temperature in the central area reached the boiling point of copper, the copper sublimated into gas in the gaseous region. As the distance from the central region increased, the laser en­ ergy decreased. When the temperature of the hemispherical shell- shaped region outside the central region was between the boiling and melting points of copper, the copper in that region melted into a liquid, forming the liquid region. Liquid copper, having low viscosity and strong fluidity, flowed into and filled part of the gaseous region, forming a flat liquid surface in a hemispherical shape. This caused the gas to be displaced above the liquid surface in the liquid region. The gaseous copper, located above the liquid region at high temperatures and pres­ sures, continued to be irradiated by the laser. When the laser intensity exceeds the breakdown threshold of gaseous copper, the copper gas is ionized into free electrons and copper ions, forming plasma. This gaseous region is thus known as the plasma region. Plasma generation occurs rapidly, and the rapid creation of high-temperature and high- pressure plasma, along with the increase in its internal pressure, can cause local plasma explosions. These explosions cause the high- temperature and high-pressure plasma to diffuse with significant ki­ netic energy. In the liquid region below, the high-temperature liquid copper undergoes a chemical reaction with oxygen in the air to produce copper oxide. Therefore, after the molten pool cools and solidifies, a layer of copper oxide forms on its surface. For the glass surface above, the high-temperature and high-pressure plasma directly impacts the glass, causing etching and increasing its surface roughness. Simulta­ neously, silicon and oxygen atoms in the glass sputter out and interact with the sputtering plasma, eventually forming a metallization layer on the glass surface. This process achieves the metallization of the glass surface. Due to the plasma sputtered at the location of the laser spot being etched away by the laser, there is no metallization layer covering the spot. The specific mechanism diagram of laser-induced plasma sputtering is shown in the Fig. 2(a). In this study, it is posited that the thickness of the plasma groups deposited on the glass surface is the thickness of the metallization layer. Simultaneously, a portion of the processed samples underwent a hydrogenation reduction treatment in a reduction furnace filled with 10 % hydrogen gas to reduce CuO to Cu. Experimental characterization After the metallization process was completed, the compactness of the metallized layer was observed using a 3D laser confocal microscope (Keyence VK-X250K, with a maximum measurement resolution of 12 nm). Observation software was used to select five different areas for each processed sample, and multifile analysis software was used to measure the surface roughness of each area. The average value was calculated as the final surface roughness for each sample. After measuring the surface roughness of the sample, the glass slide was cut open with a glass knife to expose the cross-section of the metallized layer. The thickness of the metallized layer was measured using a scanning electron microscope (SEM), and the elemental composition of the metallized layer was analyzed using an energy dispersive spec­ trometer (EDS). The surface roughness of the sample after hydrogena­ tion reduction was measured using the same method, and its thickness was measured using a 3D laser confocal microscope. In this study, it is posited that the denser the metallized layer, the smaller the surface roughness, and the thicker the layer in the processed sample, the better the processing effect. The reason why it is believed that the smaller the surface roughness of the metallization layer, the better the processing effect is to prevent the metallization layer from falling off when it rubs against other objects. 3. Experimental results and analysis The effect of different scanning speeds v on metallization processing was first discussed. A laser with a pulse width of tp = 388 fs, wavelength λ = 1040 nm, repetition frequency f = 100 kHz, scanning times N = 5, radius of the focused spot r = 18 μm and a single pulse energy of Ep = 10 μJ was used to metallize the glass surface at different scanning speeds. Each sample was scanned 5 times, with an initial downward defocus distance of 50 μm as shown in Fig. 1(a). From Figs. 6 (b), 6(d), and 6(f), it can be observed that the copper layer sputtered onto the glass surface exhibits a distinctive cluster structure. The surface is not smooth but possesses a cauliflower-like morphology with an evident layered structure. This typical structure occurs because the metallization process involves plasma sputtering deposition, which is a non-equilibrium growth process. During sputtering, the arriving copper atoms and ions possess limited surface mobility. They preferentially nucleate and aggregate at sites of higher energy, leading to the forma­ tion of isolated islands that subsequently grow in a three-dimensional manner, resulting in the observed coarse, granular clusters and the layered growth patterns [53]. To directly substantiate this claim, the morphology evolution of the deposited film was investigated from the initial stage. Fig. 7 compares the surface after 1 and 5 scanning cycles at 100 mm/s. As shown in the SEM images and corresponding EDS elemental maps of Fig. 6(a) and (c), discrete islands and tracks of copper oxide particles are observed on the glass substrate after a single scan. In contrast, after 5 scans shown in Fig. 7(b) and (d), the film evolves into a fully continuous, uniform, and dense layer, as evidenced by the complete and homogeneous copper distribution. This series provides direct evidence that the film grows via island coales­ cence, and the increased number of sputtered particles with scanning cycles is indeed responsible for the improved uniformity and density.
