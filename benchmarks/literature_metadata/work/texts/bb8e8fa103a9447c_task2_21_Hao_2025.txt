Introduction Film cooling stands as the most widely utilized external cooling technology for turbine blades, playing a critical role in isolating the high-temperature gas from direct contact and heat exchange with the metal surface while simultaneously removing a portion of the heat through convective transfer. This mechanism effectively protects tur­ bine blades from exceeding maximum allowable temperatures, thereby ensuring engine operational safety. However, conventional film cooling technology now confronts emerging challenges in balancing film cooling effectiveness with demands for enhanced cooling margin, reduced coolant consumption, and minimized energy loss – a pivotal research focus in contemporary turbine blade cooling design. To address these challenges, researchers have extensively studied various shaped hole configurations, including fan-shaped holes [1,2], laidback fan-shaped holes [3,4], sinker holes [5], and slot holes [1,6]. Nevertheless, most current in-service and under-development turbine blades predominantly employ cylindrical holes or simple expansion-type designs such as fan- shaped and laidback fan-shaped holes. This design conservatism pri­ marily stems from manufacturing limitations, particularly the technical challenges in achieving complex hole geometries while maintaining satisfactory film-cooling hole quality. The prevailing fabrication technologies for film-cooling holes pri­ marily include electrical discharge machining (EDM) and laser drilling (LD), while additive manufacturing technology (AMT, commonly termed 3D printing) demonstrates considerable developmental potential and is increasingly employed in experimental investigations of film cooling performance. However, this technique presents inherent limitations: restricted machining depth predominantly suitable for shallow and micro-hole fabrication; susceptibility to electrode deformation during feed motion, potentially inducing dimensional deviations (including hole diameter variations and circularity errors); surface roughness; and undesirable microstructural defects such as cavities and pores [7,8]. In contrast, laser drilling operates as a non-contact process that eliminates vibration and mechanical damage inherent to conventional machining. Nevertheless, this method introduces characteristic processing imper­ fections [9–11]: hole diameter inaccuracies (encompassing diameter precision and circularity deviations), edge quality degradation (man­ ifested as trumpet-shaped inlets or outlets and filleted corners), non- uniform wall roughness, as well as taper and straightness errors. AM enables fabrication of micro-scale holes and intricate geometries, mak­ ing it particularly valuable for experimental test specimens in film cooling research. However, comparative analyses [12,13] reveal inferior hole quality relative to EDM and LD, principally characterized by: amplified geometric deviations stemming from deposition-direction constraints and layer-wise stacking effects; dimensional inaccuracies induced by thermal deformation during metal solidification; and elevated surface roughness levels. The geometric deviations in film hole manufacturing, categorized as stochastic geometric errors, arise from inherent uncertainties between the as-manufactured hole morphologies and their design specifications. These deviations, causing actual film cooling effectiveness to deviate from design targets, induce significant dispersion and unpredictability in cooling performance. Current understanding of manufacturing- induced geometric variations on cooling performance remains limited. Pioneering work by Bunker’s [14] team at GE Aviation in 2000 first identified inevitable discrepancies between manufactured and designed hole geometries, particularly investigating blockage effects caused by coating spraying or particle deposition at hole exits. Their findings revealed film cooling effectiveness reductions of 50 % for cylindrical holes and 30 % for laidback fan-shaped holes. This demonstrates the critical impact of manufacturing tolerances on cooling performance. As visualized in Fig. 1 through deviation contour maps from Emma M. Veley’s study [15], laser powder bed fusion (LPBF)-manufactured laidback fan-shaped holes exhibit multiple inherent geometric imper­ fections including diameter deviations, injection angle deviations, edge filleted corner, surface roughness, and irregular exit geometries. Infrared thermography measurements demonstrated that such geo­ metric deviations could reduce film cooling effectiveness by 8 %. Complementary research by Wei Shi et al. [16] identified similar geo­ metric deviations in selective laser melting (SLM)-produced fan-shaped holes, including diameter deviations, injection angle deviations, taper angles, and exit distortions. Large eddy simulations (LES) using reverse- engineered hole models showed 10.4 % decreased film cooling effec­ tiveness compared to ideal geometries. This compilation highlights the critical significance of manufacturing tolerances in cooling performance. However, current research predominantly focuses on diffuser-type geometries (fan-shaped or laidback fan-shaped holes), while cylindrical holes – despite their higher geometric sensitivity and broader industrial applications – remain understudied. Additionally, current research on manufacturing deviations of cylindrical holes remains confined to diameter variations and expansion-type taper angles. Other subtle yet critical devia­ tions—including contraction-type taper, injection angle misalignment, and axial straightness deviations—have not been systematically char­ acterized. This gap contributes to deviations of practical film cooling effectiveness from design specifications. Hence, this study focused on cylindrical holes through systematic manufacturing and characterization. A total of 480 cylindrical holes were fabricated using two distinct processes: 210 via EDM with controlled current intensities and pulse widths on Ni3Al single-crystal superalloy substrates, and 270 via AMT with material stacking orien­ tations aligned along three orthogonal axes (hole axis, specimen longi­ tudinal direction, and specimen transverse direction). Industrial computed tomography (CT) scanning coupled with advanced image contour recognition enabled precise quantification of four typical geo­ metric parameters: diameter deviation, injection angle, taper angle and straightness error. Pressure-sensitive paint (PSP) thermographic mea­ surement at engine-representative blowing ratios (0.5–1.25) was employed to investigate the influence degree and flow and heat transfer mechanism of each geometric deviation. This paper could provide data support for the effective connection of “theoretical design − manufacturing − engineering application” chain of film holes. 2. Integrated experimental and computational approaches 2.1. Sampling for hole specimens of structural deviation To systematically investigate the flow and heat transfer mechanisms of hole typical geometric deviations on the film cooling effectiveness, two industrially relevant fabrication technologies—electrical discharge machining (EDM) and additive manufacturing technology (AMT)— were used to process film holes, which were widely used on engineering turbine blades. Firstly, Ni3Al single-crystal superalloy substrates were precision-sectioned into specimens shown as Fig. 2 (6.25 mm [length] × 6 mm [width] × 1.5 mm [height]). The height dimension replicated actual turbine blade wall thickness. The planar dimensions were suitable for industrial computed tomography (CT) scanning resolution re­ quirements (about 3 μm/ voxel spatial resolution). Each specimen was processed with 15 film holes by EDM. The hole diameter was 0.5 mm and the injection angle was 45◦, matching industrial turbine blade film holes’ geometric specifications. The feed surface was characterized by the simulated turbine blade outer aerodynamic surface. Totally, the EDM group were fabricated 14 specimens, or 210 holes. Besides, The AMT group (18 specimens, 270 holes) employed laser powder bed fusion with three material stacking orientations—axial (build direction), lengthwise, and widthwise—to comprehensively characterize hole exit filleted corner parameters. The implemented processing methods enabled to obtain geo­ metric datasets for film holes in engineering. The cross-sectional geometries of individual film holes were non- destructively characterized by high-resolution industrial CT, as depic­ ted in Fig. 2 (A-A section). Geometric deviation characterization was achieved through cross-sectional contour recognition of hole internal channels at the A-A section, combined with function fitting. The four typical geometric deviations studied in this paper were identified through this method: 1) Diameter Deviation (△D/Dref): Linear regression of inner wall profiles established two linear func­ tions, shown as the white dotted lines in Fig. 2. The minimum distance between two linear functions within the range of the hole channel was the minimum value of the hole diameter, which limited the mass flow rate of the coolant jet and was regarded as the diameter of the hole. The ratio of the difference between this diameter and the ideal cylindrical hole diameter to the ideal cylindrical hole diameter was the diameter deviation defined in this paper. The diameter deviation ratio was normalized as △D/Dref = (D − Dref)/ Dref, where Dref denoted the ideal cylindrical hole diameter (0.5 mm). 2) Injection Angle Deviation (△α): The line connecting the centers of the hole inlet and outlet formed by the two linear functions was taken as the central axis of the hole, and the angle between it and the X-axis (flow direction) was the injection angle. The difference between the recognized injection angle and the ideal cylindrical hole injection angle was the injection angle deviation defined in this paper. The injection angle deviation was indicated as △α = 45 ◦- α, where 45 ◦was the ideal cylindrical hole injection angle. 3) Taper Angle Deviation (β): Since the ideal cylindrical hole had no expansion angle, the angle between the two linear functions fitted was the taper angle deviation (β) defined in this paper. 4) Straightness Deviation (γin, γout): In particular, the straightness of film holes was rarely mentioned in previous studies, but this often occurred in the actual morphology of film holes and could not to be ignored. In order to reproduce the hole straightness in the subsequent experimental exploration, this paper divided the hole along the 1/2 wall thickness into the inlet segment and the outlet segment. According to the flow direction of the secondary flow in the hole channel, the inlet segment was closer to the secondary flow inflow, while the outlet segment was closer to the secondary outflow. Similarly, the central axes of the two segments were obtained through linear function fitting. The difference between the recognized central axis angle and the ideal cylindrical hole injection angle was the straightness deviation defined in this paper. Secondary flow-driven alignment deviations were quantified as: γin = 45 ◦- αin (inlet segment), γout = 45 ◦- αout (outlet segment), where αin and αout denoted local axis angles derived from segmented linear fits. The geometric deviations of film holes in EDM are mainly caused by electrode wear and unstable discharge gaps, while in AMT those pri­ marily result from uneven powder particle size and layer-stacking er­ rors. This parameterization framework enabled quantitative correlation between manufacturing-induced geometric imperfections and cooling performance degradation. The correlation could provide data support for the effective connection of “theoretical design − manufacturing − engineering application” chain of film holes (Fig. 3). Fig. 4 statistically quantifies the four typical geometric deviations of film holes fabricated via EDM and AMT processes. AMT-produced holes exhibited inferior geometric accuracy compared to EDM-fabricated holes, as evidenced by broader dispersion ranges of geometric de­ viations. Statistical analysis demonstrated that AMT-produced holes showed diameter deviation ratios ranging from 0 % to 40 % with non- uniform distribution, while EDM-processed holes exhibited unimodal diameter deviation ratios predominantly within 0 %-15 % (over 90 % of samples) and a maximum of 20 %. Injection angle deviations for AMT- produced holes followed near-normal distributions between −3 ◦and 3 ◦(mean: −1 ◦to 0 ◦), contrasting with EDM-fabricated holes’ asymmetric spread (−1 ◦to 3 ◦) lacking distinct distribution patterns. AMT-processed holes displayed significantly larger taper angle variations (−4 ◦to 3.5 ◦) versus EDM-fabricated holes’ range (−0.5 ◦to 3 ◦). Straightness de­ viations at outlet segments universally exceeded those at inlet segments across both manufacturing methods. AMT-processed holes exhibited hole inlet segment axis deflection angles of −3 ◦to 4 ◦and outlet segment axis deflection angles of −6 ◦to 3 ◦, both showing non-uniform distribu­ tions. EDM-fabricated holes demonstrated narrower, near-normally distributed deviations: −2 ◦to 1.5 ◦(inlet) and −3.5 ◦to 2.5 ◦(outlet), with means clustered between −1 ◦and 0 ◦. For compar­ ison, the above geometric deviations all took the value of 0 in an ideal cylindrical hole. The statistically-driven sampling strategy ensured experimental validity across 98 % of observed geometric variations, while maintaining research accuracy through representative case reduction. Experimental scheme 2.2.1. The experimental facilities Fig. 6 shows the configuration diagram of the straight channel plate film cooling effectiveness experimental installation (Fig. 6 (a)) and test section (Fig. 6 (b)), and the main experimental facilities are numerically labeled in the figure. This flat plate experimental installation could be categorized into four subsystems: the mainstream supply system, the secondary flow supply system, the imaging system, and the flat plate test section. Detailed descriptions of each subsystem were provided below: 1) The mainstream supply system: It consisted of an external rotor double-inlet fan (model: DKT4.5 15–42, power: 5.5 kW), flow straight­ ening transition duct, two-stage rectifying grids, two-layer wire gauze filters, and a bellmouth-shaped inlet. This configuration effectively broke down large-scale vortex structures generated by the fan, and ensured mainstream uniformity. A boundary layer suction slot was positioned 300 mm upstream of film holes, allowing the mainstream boundary layer to redevelop. To enforce fully-developed turbulent mainstream conditions required for Pressure-Sensitive Paint (PSP) technology (i.e., Lewis number = 1[26]), a trip wire with a diameter of 1.5 mm was installed 100 mm upstream of film holes. To eliminate interference from ambient light, a 650 nm long-pass optical filter was mounted in front of the camera lens. Each measurement sequence involved capturing 50 images at an exposure time of 25 ms, followed by grayscale averaging across all im­ ages to mitigate camera noise. 4) The flat plate test section: The experimental section was fabri­ cated from acrylic material via CNC (Computer Numerical Control) machining, with a streamwise length of 250 mm.
