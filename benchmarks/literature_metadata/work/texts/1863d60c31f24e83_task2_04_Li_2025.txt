Introduction Film-cooling holes (FCHs), renowned for their superior cooling ef- ficiency, are extensively utilized on Nickel-based single crystal super- alloy (SX) turbine blades. As a pivotal cooling technique, they introduce significant stress concentration, a focal concern in structural strength analysis. Studies of turbine blade fractures reveal that approximately 80% of failures originate from these FCHs [1-2]. The precision and quality in machining these holes directly influence the reliability of turbine blades, thereby impacting the safety of entire aviation engines. Presently, both engineering and academic communities acknowledge the peril of strength analyses detached from material and manufacturing processes, particularly critical for FCH components requiring high reli- ability under severe operational conditions. There are various methods for processing and manufacturing FCHs, each of which introduces initial damage to the FCH structure, subse- quently impacting its structural strength [3,4]. Although femtosecond laser processing technology has become more widely used in recent years, it does not fundamentally resolve this issue [5]. The fatigue phenomenon in turbine blades is particularly pronounced during the take-off, cruise, and landing phases of engine operation. As a result, the study of fatigue failure, taking into account the hole-making process, has garnered increasing attention from researchers. Zhang et al. [6] con- ducted high-cycle fatigue tests on SX superalloy FCH plates produced via various hole-making processes at 980 ◦C. Results indicated that the fa- tigue limit of holes manufactured by the EHM exceeded that of high-speed EDM and millisecond LDM by approximately 5.3% and 7.1%, respectively, despite similar fracture morphologies among the samples. Variations in high-temperature creep life due to hole-making techniques were also substantial [7.8], contrasting with less evident differences in yield strength observed in static tensile tests [9]. None- theless, significant discrepancies in yield strength were noted across different hole shapes [10]. Previous studies [11,12] have identified the recast layer surrounding the hole, microcracks within this layer, and the roughness around the hole as significant contributors to structural is- sues. Factors such as stress concentration in the FCHs and the use of non-ideal manufacturing materials effectively accelerate plastic defor- mation, as well as crack initiation and propagation [13]. These alterations, coupled with the presence of rough, un- even microcrack regions and localized element diffusion, significantly expedite crack nucleation [14]. Unfortunately, current evaluation methods for FCH quality primarily rely on qualitative or semi-qualitative descriptions and cannot utilize a single index to mea- sure initial damage. As a result, they are ineffective in accurately assessing FCH quality. The Hot Section Technology (HOST) project is the first to identify the fatigue damage failure mechanisms in SX materials, specifically linked to dislocation accumulation on octahedral slip systems [15]. This mechanism differs from the accumulation observed in other deformed superalloys [16] and directional solidification alloys [17,18]. The foundational contributions date back to 1948 when Hill introduced the orthotropic yield criterion and associated flow rules, pioneering the study of constitutive relations in anisotropic materials. Subsequently, a variety of elastic-plastic and viscoplastic constitutive models have been developed [19-21]. Building upon these frameworks, both phenome- nological macroscopic models and crystal slip theory-based models have been proposed, alongside energy-based life models tailored for two-phase composite amorphous cells [20,22,23]. However, the pre- dictive capability of phenomenological models is not as strong as that of crystal plasticity models, which involve complex variable evolution and parameter determination [24]. Crystal plasticity models effectively capture crystal anisotropy and orientation sensitivity [25]. In recent years, some studies have focused on the fracture mechanics of SX ma- terials using crystal plasticity theory. While crack propagation behavior can be simulated through the lens of microscopic material deformation, life prediction has been largely overlooked [26,27]. Overall, crystal plasticity modeling is the preferred method among researchers because of its superior ability to address these intricate aspects and predict ma- terial fatigue life. The presence of inherent porosity in SX materials can significantly influence their fatigue performance [28]. This effect is crucial in the analysis of smooth specimens but becomes negligible in structures featuring FCHs, where damage is more conspicuous. FCHs primarily impact crack initiation and propagation by altering the original surface conditions and stress distribution state of the material. This observation has been supported by Li et al. [29] and Sabnis et al. [27] through simulation analysis and digital image correlation experiments, respec- tively. Kersey et al. [30] further noted that the introduction of FCHs may reduce the thermal fatigue life of blades by 50%~75%. The character- ization of damage evolution rates related to stress concentrations relies heavily on the damage-based crystallographic local stress-strain method or critical distance theory life model [31-33]. These models incorporate physical quantities such as stress, strain, strain rate, and material de- fects, representing the current mainstream approach to studying fatigue life in relation to stress concentration. For example, Li et al. [34,35] used crystal plasticity theory to analyze circumferential stress, damage dis- tribution, and crack nucleation around holes, predicting the low-cycle fatigue life of SX superalloy while accounting for the roundness of the 2σ dispersion zone. Meanwhile, Luo et al. [24,33] and Fan et al. [36] applied the typical Manson-Coffin formula and strain energy model to examine the effects of crystal orientation, mean stress, and stress ratio, establishing a low-cycle life prediction model for turbine blades based on the critical plane method. Describing fatigue damage in SX superalloys with FCH structures at different temperatures is notably complex. This complexity arises mainly from the close relationship between material damage and factors such as temperature, load (stress ratio, frequency, etc.), and material crystal orientation [37,38]. Additionally, the initial damage of the FCH remains difficult to quantify [39]. High temperatures significantly in- fluence the fatigue deformation mechanisms of SX materials [40.41]. Research by Pei et al. [42] on transient thermal fatigue due to temperature changes from 25 ◦C to 1050 ◦C indicates that these tem- perature fluctuations further alter the failure mechanisms at hole edges. Moreover, conducting a unified analysis of fatigue life across different temperatures has proven to be a challenging task. It’s essential to un- derstand that predicting fatigue life while considering the initial damage from hole fabrication is crucial for assessing the strength of single crystal blades. Unfortunately, there has been a notable lack of reporting on this subject to date. The equivalent initial flaw size (EIFS) theory offers a quantitative approach to assessing initial damage in various structural components [43]. Its central premise is to treat the initial damage of any material or structure as an imaginary crack, allowing for the prediction of fatigue life under different loading conditions based on the concept of damage tolerance [44,45]. Grounded in established fracture mechanics, this theory incorporates initial damage assessments into overall fatigue life predictions, thus eliminating the need for direct examination of small cracks. Several studies [46,47] have systematically investigated methods for deriving EIFS values and predicting fatigue life, primarily focusing on crack propagation at specific temperatures. Notably, Sha- hani et al. [48] sought to enhance life prediction by correlating reverse-derived EIFS values with loading stresses, which contradicts the initial assumption that EIFS is independent of test conditions. On the other hand, the thermal sensitivity of the matrix and strengthening phases in SX materials adds variability [49,50], compli- cating the determination of EIFS using traditional retro-calculation methods. In terms of crack propagation, Tina’s research highlights the inadequacy of isotropic stress intensity factor solutions and projected crack lengths for angled cracks in SX superalloys [51]. Several re- searchers have suggested modifications to standard stress intensity factor solutions for better describing crack propagation [52,53], while others have explored the microscopic mechanisms of crack growth. For instance, Karamitros et al. [54] studied short crack growth in micro- structures using CPFEM and XFEM; however, these methods are not universally applicable due to differences in pore side slip between FCH structures and smooth materials, making crack growth description quite complex [55]. Furthermore, the EIFS theory has shown a strong ability to predict fatigue life by incorporating manufacturing factors in previous studies [29,43]. However, there is an urgent need to validate its pre- dictive capability for FCH structures across a range of temperatures, from room temperature (R.T.) to elevated levels, as the initial damage during manufacturing is independent of temperature, while different segments of turbine blade FCHs often operate under varying thermal conditions. To address these challenges, this paper introduces an EIFS calcula- tion model and life prediction approach in Section 2, synthesizing existing research comprehensively. Section 3 presents crack propagation experiments on SX superalloy plates with FCH manufactured using the same process, spanning from R.T. to elevated levels, thereby yielding dependable EIFS results and fatigue crack growth rates that account for crack closure effects. Finally, Section 4 forecasts and assesses total fa- tigue life by integrating effective EIFS values and obtained crack growth rates. This study aims to offer novel insights into the practical applica- tion of EIFS in turbine blade FCH structures. 2. Method This section presents a comprehensive framework for predicting the fatigue life of SX materials with FCHs considering initial damage, con- sisting of four key components. First, it treats initial damage in the FCHs as the EIFS, developing a model that adapts EIFS solutions from smooth to FCHs specimens while considering stress concentration effects. Next, it introduces an incremental plasticity J-integral as the fatigue crack propagation driving force, based on crack propagation data from SX materials [56,57]. The framework then incorporates the small crack propagation closure effect to account for temperature sensitivity and micro-crack behavior, refining the crack growth driving force. Finally, it F. Small crack propagation closure effect At elevated temperatures, SX materials demonstrate a fatigue mechanism influenced by oxygen, fatigue crack closure induced by oxidation. This phenomenon involves the formation of an oxide layer within the crack, which is approximately as thick as the crack opening displacement (COD) in Fig. 5. This oxide layer diminishes the driving force for crack growth, consequently slowing down the rate of crack propagation. Typically, the oxide within the crack is believed to form via "fretted oxidation", wherein the oxide continually fractures and reforms under cyclic loading, thereby establishing a continuous external oxide layer. For the analysis of the continuous oxidation closure effect, we introduce a simplified model for COD characterized by the following assumptions: (i) The maximum displacement, denoted as δmax, is solely dependent on the maximal load and remains unaffected by oxidation processes. (ii) The minimum displacement, δmin, equates to the thickness of the external oxide layer, under the presumption that the oxide be- haves rigidly. (iii) An average oxide thickness is considered within a 10 μm region following the crack tip, where oxygen penetration into the material forms an internal oxide, exerting negligible influence on crack opening. A single orientation is ensured on the blank, and dog-bone shaped flat specimens are prepared, with the designed geometric di- mensions and actual processed specimen features shown in Figs. 6 and 7, respectively. The thickness of the specimen is 1.0 mm, the diameter of the FCH is 0.5 mm, and femtosecond laser drilling path is in a spiral cutting manner. The processed FCHs meet the geometric circularity and feature characteristics (Fig. 6 (a)). The beam processing process is depicted in Fig. 6 (b), which is a complete surface processing with multiple turns, with each layer taking approximately 4.44 s to process and a single layer feed of 0.1 mm.
