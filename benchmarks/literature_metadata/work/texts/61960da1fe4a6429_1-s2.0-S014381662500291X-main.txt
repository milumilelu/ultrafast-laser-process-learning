In order to optimize the laser drilling quality in a 200 μm thick Borofloat (BF) 33 glass substrate, processing parameters such as laser pulse duration, laser pulse energy, number of passes and TGV hole diameter are studied in detail. After laser drilling, through-holes are evaluated for the hole diameter, taper angle and crack formation using optical microscopy and scanning electron microscopy. Although small taper angles down to 6 ± 0.3° are obtained using a femtosecond pulse duration, microcracks and backside ablation of the glass substrate are observed. Using a laser pulse duration of 5 ps, a laser pulse energy of 25 μJ and 600 passes for TGV fabrication with a hole diameter of 125 μm, a minimum taper angle of down to 7.3 ± 0.1° is obtained without the formation of cracks. Minimum hole pitches of 180 μm are realized for a hole diameter of 125 μm. To meet industrial requirements for temperature resistance in electronics manufacturing, laser-drilled TGV arrays are tested for thermal sensitivity using a furnace for both thermal cycling and shock at temperatures up to 950°. After thermal tests, no crack formation is observed for a minimum hole pitch of 180 μm and maximal laser­ induced stresses is reduced by 37.4% down to 8.2 MPa on average, demonstrating the great potential of the applied scanning strategy for glass-based core packaging. 1. Introduction Driven by the ongoing progress towards more eﬃcient and power­ ful wearable electronics, smart cars, advanced sensors, medical devices and communication networks, the increasing performance of high-end packaging in electronics manufacturing is critical [1--3]. For advanced 2.5D and 3D electronic packaging, glass-based cores and packages as well as interposers have gained particular interest as a good alternative to commonly used materials such as silicon, ceramics or organic lami­ nates [4--7]. In addition to its availability at low cost, excellent chemical stability and biocompatibility, glass material has superior electrical and thermal properties (e.g. low dielectric loss, high thermal stability, high resistivity, adjustable thermal expansion coeﬃcient) and lower para­ sitic capacitance compared to conventional silicon and SOI substrates [1--3,8--10]. * Corresponding author. E-mail address: Daniel.Franz@th-ab.de (D. Franz). Metallized through-hole glass vias (TGVs) are high-density and high­ aspect ratio vertical interconnects embedded in glass substrates in elec­ tronic devices, enabling complex structural designs through a multi­ layer circuit integration. TGVs allow shorter electrical paths than hori­ zontal ones, which is crucial for the ongoing miniaturization and thus the required power density in microelectronics [4,11]. Along with var­ ious other etching methods, electrochemical discharge machining, ul­ trasonic drilling, powder blasting and mechanical drilling, laser drilling is the most widely used technology for forming through holes in glass materials [12,13]. In most cases, pulsed CO2 laser and excimer laser sources with wavelengths of 𝜆= 10,600 nm and 193 nm, respectively, are used [14--17]. Broas et al. [18] use both laser sources for drilling of 60 μm and 70 μm TGVs in polymer-laminated glass substrates with a thickness of 100 μm. It has been shown that characteristic defects such as cracks in the heat-affected zone, are less pronounced after thermal tests. A well-known disadvan­ tage of the infrared wavelength of the CO2 laser and the relatively long laser pulse duration for laser drilling of glass is thermally induced ab­ lation, causing residual stress in the drilling area. In turn, these favours crack formation during the cooling process or at any time later, reducing reliability of electrical interconnections of metallized TGVs [19]. Brus­ berg et al. [20] overcame thermo-mechanically induced stresses of CO2 laser drilling in various glass substrates with thicknesses from 145 μm to 500 μm by thermal post-treatment of TGV arrays with hole pitches down to 150 μm. However, absolutely crack-free drilling of through holes in glass could not be demonstrated. In contrast, excimer lasers with a wavelength in the UV-C range are generally suitable for high throughput parallel processing and high density of small, high quality holes through the use of masks. [10,14,15]. For example, laser drilling of 30 μm thick alkali-free glass with minimum hole sizes of 5.5 μm and pitches of 10 μm has been demonstrated without cracking or edge damage, enabling high density and throughput in laser drilling for elec­ tronic packaging applications [15]. However, the industrial application of mask-based excimer laser TGV drilling is restricted by the limited power availability, the maintenance of the UV optics, the increased safety precautions required for both fluorine and chlorine and the in­ ﬂexibility to new layouts [5,21,22]. Recently, ultrashort pulsed (USP) laser with pulse durations from tens of picoseconds down to femtoseconds have become of particular interest for laser glass drilling applications due to their special laser ma­ terial interaction [2,11,23--29]. Although the absorption rate of glass is low in the visible spectral range, the short pulse duration enable TGV formation by multiphoton absorption [3]. Thermal eﬀects in laser mi­ cromachining with small focal diameters and high repetition rates are negligible due to so-called cold ablation processes [21]. Matsumoto et al. [24] use 28 ps pulse trains for laser drilling of AF32 eco thin glass less than 100 μm thick. The authors successfully demonstrate the fab­ rication of zero taper TGVs with a diameter of approximately 10 μm, smooth sidewalls and minimal spatter. The short processing time of 20 μs also demonstrates the great potential for high-throughput fabrica­ tion for advanced electronic packaging, although experimental studies are still required. To avoid the potential for cracking after laser drilling due to thermally induced stress in subsequent processes, the authors ap­ plied a post-process heat treatment [26]. In addition to the conventional use of single pulse mode for percussion drilling, the GHz burst mode with nanosecond pulse-to-pulse delays is used to produce high quality blind vias with smooth sidewalls in a variety of glasses [27--32]. How­ ever, the use of the GHz burst mode for TGV fabrication has not been suﬃciently investigated. To overcome the existing challenges of direct laser drilling for TGV manufacturing, such as slow processing speeds and micro-crack for­ mation, an etching step is often applied after laser processing [33]. Selective laser-induced etching (SLE) applies an innovative combina­ tion of USP laser-based ablation or modification and a subsequent wet etching process using solvents such as KOH or HF to produce small and high aspect ratio through-holes, enabling the generation of crack-free holes with excellent sidewall quality at high processing speeds [33--41]. However, in TGV production using the SLE process, the throughput is di­ rectly dependent on the number of holes previously laser ablated and the applied etching solutions have a negative impact on environmental sus­ tainability. It should also be noted that although the laser modified area is etched faster, the solution attacks the entire glass substrate, which means that the initial material cost is higher and the process is limited in its application. For this reason, a single-stage USP laser-based drilling process is always preferred, which is particularly interesting for low­ density glass-based core packaging applications with TGV densities typi­ cally below a few hundred vias/cm2, such as in micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS) technology, Lab-on-a-Chip (LoC) or microfluidic sys­ tems [1,10,42--44]. Against this background, we report on a comprehensive study of TGV laser drilling in Borofloat (BF) 33 glass substrate using an USP laser with a wavelength of 515 nm to determine its potential for glass­ based electronic packaging. The drilling quality is analyzed in terms of the hole diameter, taper angle and crack formation using diﬀerent laser pulse durations, laser pulse energies and number of passes. Since post-drilling residual stresses are conducive to crack formation, these are determined for TGV arrays with diﬀerent hole sizes and pitches using imaging polarimetry. In order to fully evaluate the application potential of the single-step drilling process for glass-based electronic packaging, the residual stresses in the glass substrate are assessed after thermal cy­ cling and shock tests. 2. Experiments 2.1. Setup Experimental investigations of laser drilling are carried out using a USP laser (Carbide CB3-80W + CBM03-2H, Light Conversion) emitting at a wavelength of 𝜆= 515 nm and a laser pulse repetition rate of 𝑓𝑝= 100 kHz. After passing through a beam expander, the circular polarized laser radiation is focused by a telecentric F-Theta lens (LINOS F-Theta-Ronar 515-540 nm, Qioptiq) with a focal length of 𝑓= 100 mm to a laser focus diameter of 𝑑= 16.5 μm (1/e2), which was measured by a CMOS image sensor (BeamPro LP6.3, Femto Easy) with a pixel size of 1.45 μm. The position of the laser focus is set to the surface of the glass substrate and is not changed during the laser drilling experiments. A 2D galvanometer scanner (ExcelliSCAN 14, Scanlab) is used for flexible positioning of the laser within the focal plane, see Fig. 1(a). In order to optimize the laser drilling quality, a series of experiments were performed using diﬀerent laser pulse energies (𝐸= 10 μJ - 30 μJ) and laser pulse durations (𝜏= 0.19 ps - 5 ps). The laser pulse energy was calculated from 𝐸= 𝑓𝑝⋅𝑃𝑎𝑣after measuring of the averaged laser power 𝑃𝑎𝑣and passing through the F-theta lens using a power meter (UP12E-70W-H5-DO, Gentec) with an uncertainty of ± 1%. An autocor­ relator (pulseCheck 50, APE Angewandte Physik und Elektronik) with an uncertainty of ± 0.02% was used to determine the laser pulse dura­ tion using a Gaussian fit function at the FWHM level. Diﬀerent scanning strategies are used for laser drilling of varying TGV diameters in the range of 125 μm to 500 μm. The laser focus is deflected from the outer to the inner scan line along up to 10 scan lines with a line-to-line dis­ tance of 𝐷= 9 μm, see Fig. 1(b). A constant scan speed of 428 mm/s results in a laser pulse overlap of 𝑂= 66%, which ensures a spatially homogeneous energy distribution [45]. For the generation of through holes in the glass substrate, the scan path architecture is repeated for a number of passes in a range of 110 to 1200. During the TGV laser drilling experiments with a hole diameter of 500 μm, minor and lo­ calized backside ablation was identified for using a constant starting point of the scan lines. In order to avoid this side eﬀect, for the re­ maining TGV drilling studies, the starting point was evenly distributed across the individual scan lines, as depicted in Fig. 1(b). In addition, the maximum number of scan lines is modified according to the TGV diam­ eter, with the outer scan line diameter always 18 μm smaller than the target hole diameter. For example, when laser drilling a 125 μm hole diameter, a maximum of 6 scan lines were used, as shown in the Ta­ ble 1. Please note that for large number of diﬀerent laser parameters, small deviations between the achieved diameter and the target diameter are observed. For the sake of clarity, the target hole diameter is used in experimental investigations to identify the influence of diﬀerent laser parameters on laser drilling quality. Nevertheless, a detailed overview of the hole diameter measurements in dependence of the applied laser parameters can be found in Tables 4, 5 and 6 in the Appendix. To assess the eﬀect on the quality of TGV laser drilling, 3 holes were drilled for each set of laser parameters.

However, it is worth noting that for applying a laser pulse energy of 30 μJ, crack formation occurs after laser drilling regardless of the laser pulse duration, although backside ablation is only visible for femtosecond laser pulses. In general, backside ablation is associated with refraction of the laser beam from the sidewall, diffraction from the ablation edge and interference between the back and front surfaces of the glass [55,56]. In the experiments using femtosecond laser pulses for the TGV formation process, ablation on the back surface of BF 33 glass occurs due to the signiﬁcantly higher pulse peak intensity compared to the picosecond laser pulse duration. In comparison, at a lower laser pulse energy of 20 μJ, cracking and backside ablation were only observed for a laser pulse duration of 𝜏= 0.19 ps - 0.5 ps. This is in contrast to the commonly known cause of crack formation, where laser ablation creates stress from the rapid heat­ ing and subsequent non-uniform cooling of the material [26,57,58]. In turn, this thermally induced stress in the glass substrate is signiﬁcantly more pronounced for using a longer laser pulse duration in the picosec­ ond range. We assume that the formation of cracks during femtosecond laser drilling is the result of the glass substrate being modified by the observed local backside ablation with depths of a few microns. These modified regions in turn lead to diﬀerent thermal gradients during laser drilling, thus facilitating cracking from the drilling edge along the cir­ cular backside ablations around the TGVs, cf. Fig. 2(d) left. In addition, Fig. 2(c) shows that the produced taper angle of drilled holes decreases with shorter laser pulse durations, except when a laser pulse duration of 0.19 ps - 0.5 ps and a laser pulse energy of 30 μJ is used. Hole diameter and number of passes In addition, to evaluate the potential and flexibility of the scanning strategy used for the TGV manufacturing process in future glass core packaging, the hole size was varied in a range from 100 μm to 400 μm. The fabricated taper angle in dependence of the TGV diameter is de­ picted in Fig. 3(a) using a laser pulse energy of 20 μJ and 25 μJ with 150 passes. First, it should be noted that for both laser fluences com­ bined with a laser pulse duration of 5 ps, no cracks were observed by optical microscopy at the edge or in the vicinity of the drilled holes. It is noticeable that the taper angle is reduced by an average of 1.4° down to 9.6° for applying a higher laser pulse energy. For both laser fluences, the steepness of the hole walls is constant for hole diameters greater than 200 μm and decreases steadily for smaller TGV diameters. From a hole diameter of less than 100 μm, the applied laser scanning strategy with 150 passes and less than 5 scan lines is not suitable for creating through-holes with a circular hole outlet. For the applied drilling strat­ egy and the 200 μm thick BF 33 glass substrate, the minimum achievable hole diameter is approximately 78 μm, which can be calculated from the average measured taper angle of 11° for laser drilling of 100 μm large TGVs, cf. Fig. 3(a). Optical microscope images are shown in Fig. 3(c) contrasting the drilling quality at the entrance and exit of the hole for diﬀerent TGV di­ ameters using a laser pulse energy of 25 μJ and 150 passes, where the increasing taper angle is also clearly visible for hole diameters below 200 μm. Overall, no cracking or backside ablation occurs during the experiments with diﬀerent hole sizes and high hole quality with high circularity is achieved even for small TGVs. A small and constant heat aﬀected zone around the exit hole is also observed for all hole sizes, although spatter due to redeposited glass from the hole into the sur­ rounding material is only visible for diameters of 300 μm and 400 μm. Minor chipping at the hole entrances is observed in both optical mi­ croscopy and SEM imaging of a 200 μm large TGV, see Fig. 3(d). In addition, the SEM images exemplify the excellent drilling quality using a laser pulse energy of 20 μJ and 150 passes and a laser pulse dura­ tion of 5 ps, which is particularly evident when considering the smooth and homogeneous sidewall. Only minor re-deposited glass substrate and debris are visible, which cannot be removed by cleaning.
