However, there is lack of research implementing GHz bursts in bottom-up processing, which is credible even for cm-thick glass processing as straight walls can be formed. In this work, GHz burst regimes consisting of more than 20 pulses were investigated for soda-lime glass bottom-up cutting. The highest cutting speed of 4.2 mm/s and linear cutting efficiency of 0.13 mm/J were achieved when cutting 4.8 mm-thick glass with picosecond pulses in GHz bursts at optimized conditions. In addition, we have demonstrated cutting of glass with thickness ranging from 1 mm to 18.8 mm, an oblique cylinder with a 35-degree slant angle, and complex contour cutting. The results were compared with the state-of-the-art. To the best of our knowledge, we have shown the highest cutting speed and efficiency compared to other studies on subtractive laser cutting of 1 mm thickness glass. Furthermore, alternative figures of merit were used to compare the reported cutting studies of various thickness glass. The relative cutting speed and efficiency achieved in this study were shown to be up to two orders of magnitude higher compared to the studies using ≈1 µm wavelength. Extensive cutting capabilities together with high cutting speeds make bottom-up cutting with GHz bursts a highly compelling technique for glass processing. 1. Introduction New advances in ultrashort lasers enable new features such as MHz and GHz burst mode operation. The burst regime for the laser machining process was highly explored after Kerse et al. published “ablation cooled” material removal using MHz and GHz bursts [1]. High repetition rate allowed the authors to ablate the material before the residual heat deposited by previous pulses had diffused away from the processing region. As a result, the thermal damage and ablation threshold were reduced, and material removal efficiency was increased. The burst regime was shown to improve the machining of metals [2], semi- conductors [3,4], polymers [5,6] and dielectrics [7,8]. The milling ef- ficiency of fused silica was improved 2 times with the use of MHz bursts [9]. The GHz bursts had an even greater effect as the milling efficiency was increased 7 times compared to the single pulse regime [10]. With more pulses per burst, the gentle ablation mechanism changed to a coarse ablation dominated by breaking out of the surface. The improved material removal rate and the increase of surface roughness for a higher number of pulses per burst were also demonstrated for milling soda-lime glass and sapphire [11]. Most of the works employ top-down cutting (TDC) as it is a universal technique used for various materials [12]. However, the ablation effi- ciency is limited as removed particles and generated plasma interact with the beam, shielding it from the sample [13,14]. Moreover, with increasing depth, the machined walls form a V-shape due to an enlarged beam spot area on the sidewalls and increased Fresnel reflections both resulting in lower fluence [15,16]. Therefore, the machining time drastically increases for thicker materials, as higher cutting widths must be used [17]. The beam interaction with the already milled walls can be avoided and straight walls can be formed by bottom-up cutting (BUC) [18]. Moreover, BUC is a more efficient process due to a lower damage threshold, emerging from a constructive interference between the inci- dent and reflected light [19]. Furthermore, the energy is more efficiently transferred to the material as the plasma formed at the rear surface heats up the surrounding material increasing its absorption [20]. The ab- sorption front moves into the bulk, generating partially confined su- perheated material [21]. For the aforementioned reasons, bottom-up machining is credible even for glasses thicker than 1 cm [23, 24], whereas TDC is typically used to cut materials up to 1 mm thickness [17,22]. BUC of glass using nanosecond pulses is a common technique for complex shapes as high milling efficiency is achieved due to laser- induced cracking [25]. As GHz bursts also indicate a similar ablation mechanism in glass [9,10], comparable bottom-up milling efficiencies are expected. However, a GHz burst laser would be more universal as gentle and coarse ablation mechanisms could be induced with the same laser. In this study, the GHz burst effect on laser cutting of soda-lime glass was investigated. The study was done using different burst re- gimes, burst energies, pulse durations, vertical speeds and distances between the pulses and scanning lines. The results were compared with the state-of-the-art and the developed technology was applied for cut- ting complex contours. 2. Experiment and methods 2.1. Laser characteristics The experiment was carried out with Carbide CB3–40 W femto- second laser (Light Conversion) emitting 1030 nm central wavelength. The pulse duration could be chosen between 194 fs – 20 ps (FWHM in single pulse regime). The laser could generate bursts of several pulses separated by 404 ps (2.5 GHz). To achieve the highest burst energy, the burst repetition rate was set to the minimal – 200 kHz (temporal sepa- ration of 5 µs). In this study, we intended to push the laser burst parameters beyond standard limits unlocking more than 10 pulses per burst. Due to our laser software/hardware limitations, only GHz bursts up to 30 pulses were used to ensure a safe laser operation. Additional temporal measurements were performed to check the burst envelope form at the non-standard operation. The temporal characteristics of bursts were measured by the detector (D-15ir, Picometrix) and oscilloscope (DPO72004C, Tek- tronix). The bursts consisting of 25 (P25) and 30 (P30) pulses were investigated (Fig. 1). In both cases, an additional pre-burst was formed before the original. The pre-burst consisted of 10 and 15 pulses for P25 and P30 burst regimes, respectively. The time between the last intra- burst pulses of the additional and the original bursts was 16 ns. As the effect of the additional burst is not known, the results in this work are provided regarding only the full burst energy. It should be noted that there was no additional pre-burst with the laser manufacturer provided standard regimes up to 10 pulses per burst. 2.2. Cutting setup The scheme of the experimental setup is illustrated in Fig. 2(a). Galvanometric scanners (IntelliSCAN 14, Scanlab) were used to position the laser beam in the X and Y axes. The Z position of the scanners together with the 100 mm focal length telecentric f-theta lens was controlled with a linear motor stage (8MT165, Standa). A zero-order λ/4 waveplate was inserted in the beam path in order to change the beam polarization from linear to circular. Moreover, the beam diameter of 4 mm was additionally increased 2 times with a variable beam expander (S6EXZ5310/328, Sill optics), resulting in the numerical aperture of 0.04. 2.3. Laser cutting methodology The beam waist diameter was 29 µm at the 1/e2 intensity level measured with the Liu’s method [26]. The focal position on the front surface was found by forming craters in different Z positions. To position the laser focus on the rear surface, the laser beam was lowered by the thickness of a sample divided by the refractive index. 4.8 mm thickness 100 × 100 mm2 soda-lime glass samples were used in the experiment. 20 mm diameter circles were cut from samples using a spiral scanning algorithm shown in Fig. 2(b). In the XY plane, the laser beam was scanned continuously inwards and outwards with respect to the circle center with an additional repetition of the inner and outer circles. In the further text, the distance between the sequent scanning lines and the distance between the sequent craters in a scanning tra- jectory will be referred to as pitch. The cutting kerf was slightly changed, depending on the pitch value. To sustain the continuous beam scanning without any jumps, the kerf had to be the multiple of the pitch. Therefore, the kerf value was equal to 200 µm or slightly higher with the least difference of the pitch multiple and 200 µm. The maximum kerf value of 225 µm was used with a 45 µm pitch. The 200 µm kerf was chosen as it was wide enough for the dust to escape and not clog the channel. At first, the laser beam waist was positioned slightly lower than the rear surface of the sample to avoid sample cracking and further destruction. The additional offset depended on the burst energy and varied between 50 µm and 1.2 mm. After positioning the beam waist, scanning proceeded, and the laser focus was lifted simultaneously with a constant vertical speed (Fig. 2(b)). Higher vertical speed corresponds to the larger removed particle size [25] and higher cutting speed. The experiment was done with P25 and P30 burst regimes using different pulse duration (5 – 20 ps), burst energy (<182 µJ), pitch (5 – 45 µm) and vertical speed (< 0.3 mm/s). For every pulse duration and burst energy combination, pitch and vertical speed were optimized, requiring approximately 8 experiments. Optimal pitch allowed reaching the highest vertical speed resulting in the shortest cutting duration. Sections 3.1 and 3.2 present the results using the optimized parameters. 2.4. Sample characterization The quality of the cut was evaluated by measuring the chippings on top and bottom surfaces with an optical microscope (5x objective, 0.15 NA, 0.96 µm/pixel, Eclipse LV100NDA, Nikon) and sidewall roughness with an optical profilometer (20x objective, 0.45 NA, 1.4 µm/pixel, S Neox, Sensofar). The linear profiles were measured along the sample perimeter positioning the line at half the sample thickness. Linear pro- files were measured using the cut-off wavelengths of 0.8 µm (for Ra < 2 µm) and 2.5 µm (for Ra > 2 µm) and evaluation length of 18 mm, following the recommendations suggested in ISO 4288. The machining process was evaluated using the inner radius (r) of the cut (Fig. 2(b)), the thickness of the sample (h) (Fig. 2(b)), cutting time (t) and laser average power (P). It should be noted that t corresponded to the time measured from the start to the end of the cut. The pitch was changed by 5 µm for every set. It should be noted that for some regimes at the highest burst energies the optimal pitch value may not have been reached because of the maximum pitch value of 45 µm. This limitation has emerged from the maximum scanning speed of galvanometer scanners equal to 9 m/s and the used burst repetition rate of 200 kHz. Therefore, it may be that more advanced scanners would result in even higher cutting speed and efficiency. For most of the cases, the optimal pitch got higher as the burst energy increased. Larger energy pulses affect a greater area of the sample [26], therefore, a larger distance is needed to sustain the overlapping of the laser-affected areas. The cutting speed dependence on the burst energy is shown in Fig. 4. For longer pulse durations, higher burst energy was needed to achieve the highest cutting speed. It is not clear if the tendency repeats for the P30 regime with 20 ps pulse duration due to the limited burst energy. The highest cutting speed of 4.2 mm/s was achieved with 180 µJ energy bursts using P30 regime and 15 ps duration. A lower number of pulses per burst led to a smaller cutting speed. For the P25 regime, the highest cutting speed of 2.4 mm/s was achieved using 10 ps pulse duration and 140 µJ. The linear cutting efficiency dependence on the burst energy is shown in Fig. 5. However, this does not repeat for the P30 burst regime, as 1.6 times higher efficiency was achieved with 10 ps compared to 5 ps. An alternative approach would be to optimize the burst repetition rate at the parameter set with the highest cutting efficiency. This would result in higher scanning speeds for lower burst energies while maintaining the optimal pitch. Therefore, if pitch is not limited by the laser beam scanning system, the highest cutting speed should be achieved with P30 with 10 ps pulses and increased burst repetition rate exploiting the full laser power. 3.2. Cutting quality The sidewall surface topographies of samples cut with P30 burst regime and sidewall roughness (Ra) dependence on burst energy are shown in the supplementary material Fig. S1 and Fig. S2, respectively. No clear surface roughness dependence on burst energy or pulse dura- tion was observed in this study for pulse duration below 20 ps. The average sidewall roughness Ra of all the samples was 2.3 µm with a standard deviation of 0.9 µm. This is comparable to the sidewall roughness of 3.2 µm (Ra) of soda-lime samples cut with nanosecond pulses [22]. The largest surface chippings on both sample sides were measured as shown in Fig. 6(a). One sample was investigated for each parameter set. No clear dependence on different burst regimes was detected. However, a higher number of samples should be taken into account as the for- mation of cracks is a stochastic phenomenon. The chippings dependence on burst energy for top and bottom sur- faces is shown in Fig. 6(b) for all the burst regimes. The top surface had on average 4.4 times larger chippings compared to the bottom surface. If we take all parameter sets into account, the average chippings for the bottom and top surfaces were 90 µm (σstdev = 50 µm) and 400 µm (σstdev = 400 µm), respectively. The chipping lengths for the rear surface are similar to the study conducted with nanosecond pulses [25]. The reason for the larger crack formation on the top surface could be hidden in the cutting process itself. The cutting starts with the focal spot being raised towards the front surface. As the cutting process goes on, the beam spot on the front surface gets smaller and energy fluence in- creases. When the beam waist comes close to the front surface, the laser fluence can exceed the damage threshold causing the front surface ablation [27,28]. The same problem was encountered by Dudutis et al. where the front surface ablation and the shattering of a thin glass layer were proposed to be responsible for the formation of large cracks [22]. The chipping size should decrease by using a higher numerical aperture as it would allow positioning the beam waist closer to the front surface before damaging it. 3.3. Comparison to other studies It was chosen to only compare the subtractive glass cutting tech- nologies all being single-step processes capable of cutting complex contours. Alternatively, the single-pass Bessel scribing can be applied for both ultrathin (100 µm) and thick (1 cm) material cutting [29,30]. Furthermore, ~m/s scribing speeds can be achieved while maintaining the separated glass sidewall quality of 2.3 µm (Ra) [31]. The high-quality sidewall with surface roughness of 0.3 µm (Sa) can be reached with the use of GHz bursts [32]. However, the Bessel beam-assisted glass cutting was excluded from the comparison as it typically is a two-step process limited to simple contours. The bottom-up (BUC) and top-down cutting (TDC) techniques are named with the first word indicating the sample surface to which the beam is focused and the second word – to the material removal direc- tion. Both BUC and TDC techniques were discussed in the introduction. In water-assisted top-down cutting (WATDC), a sample can be sub- merged into still water [33] or a flowing water layer could be formed on the top surface of the sample [17]. In this way, the debris shielding is reduced as the ablated materials are removed more efficiently due to a higher plasma pressure, stronger shockwaves and the impact of the cavitation bubbles [22]. Furthermore, steeper walls are formed [34] and narrower cutting widths can be used [22]. Moreover, water removes the excessive heat from the interaction zone more effectively as it has a higher specific heat capacity than air. Therefore, higher laser power can be used without shattering the sample, increasing the cutting throughput [35]. Furthermore, improved heat dissipation was also shown to increase sample flexural strength [22]. Water could also contribute as a buffer layer for inducing filamentation used for cutting [15]. The cutting speed is calculated either by dividing the cutting length by the processing time [36] or by dividing the scanning speed by the number of cut lines [17,22,33–35,37]. In the latter case, the time spent on jumps between the scanning lines is not taken into account. Straight [15,17,22,33–35,37], circular [33] and rectangular [22,36] cutting trajectories are demonstrated in different studies. Samples of various thickness are being used in different laser glass- cutting studies. The comparison of linear cutting efficiency and speed achieved at different studies could be only applied to the same thickness samples as they are directly proportional to it. Therefore, 2.3 mm and 1 mm thickness glasses were additionally cut using 225 µm cutting kerf. The cutting speed and linear cutting efficiency of different laser cutting techniques for 1 mm thickness samples are shown in Fig. 7. By comparing the results achieved with ≈1 µm wavelength, the relative cutting speed reported in this study exceeds others by 8 to 134 times, while the relative cutting efficiency is 3.2 – 64 times higher. However, 532 nm ns-BUC is a much more competitive method. Still, the relative cutting efficiency reported in this study is slightly higher, while the relative cutting speed is about twice as fast as 532 nm ns-BUC. The relative cutting speed and efficiency values shown in Table 1 are also depicted in Fig. 9(a, b). The values achieved in this study are the highest in some cases being two orders of magnitude larger than others. The proposed method allowed achieving comparable relative cutting speed and efficiency for samples with thickness reduced to 1 mm. However, cutting speed typically does not depend linearly on glass thickness. Therefore, higher cutting speed and efficiency could be ob- tained for thinner glass with the reduced kerf. Furthermore, the effi- ciency of top-down techniques would be even lower if scaled up to a larger thickness. The 532 nm ns-BUC led to the highest relative cutting speed for single pulse regimes. Shorter wavelengths result in higher nonlinear absorption and nanosecond pulses induce larger thermal load to the sample. This results in material cracking, which is more efficient than ablation [22,23]. This study shows that high cutting speeds can also be achieved with picosecond pulses in the GHz burst mode. Soda-lime glass has a low thermal diffusivity (0.5 mm2/s [39]) which leads to µs-scale thermal relaxation time for ~µm lengths of interest. As the duration between the intra-burst pulses is short (404 ps), subsequent pulses in- crease the thermal load which leads to the cracking of the material. In addition, higher peak intensity of picosecond pulses leads to an increased nonlinear absorption. The pulse peak intensity is increased a thousand times when comparing picoseconds to nanoseconds. On the contrary, the pulse division into a burst reduces the peak intensities of single pulses only by the number of intra-burst pulses. Therefore, GHz burst mode with picosecond pulses takes advantage of a higher ab- sorption together with a higher thermal load. This leads to a consider- able increase in the cutting speed. The cutting quality in terms of the sidewall roughness (Ra) and sur- face damage is depicted in Fig. 9(c, d) and Table 1. It should be noted that the front and bottom surface damage widths are separated in Table 1, the highest of which is shown in Fig. 9(d). No clear dependence for sample thickness is seen in Fig. 9(c, d). However, the BUC technique leads to lower quality compared to the top-down approaches. 3.4. Applications The bottom-up cutting is a universal technique that can be used for complex shapes (Fig. 10(a–c)). Furthermore, processing of extremely thick materials can be done as shown in Fig. 10(d) where an 18.8 mm thickness sample was cut. In addition, not only straight cuts of 0◦but also slant angles up to 35◦can be accomplished (Fig. 10(f)). The slanted sample was cut by translating the cutting zone by Δx at different Z po- sitions while the beam angle of incidence remained the same (Fig. 10 (e)). However, we note that the sidewall roughness increased with the slant angle (Fig. S3 in the supplementary material), reaching 5.7 µm (Ra) at 35◦. It should be noted that the relative cutting speeds were lower for all of the samples shown in the Fig. 10. The cutting widths had to be enlarged for thick and slanted samples to facilitate the removal of ab- lated particles. In addition, the burst repetition rates were lowered for samples shown in Fig. 10(a, d). For thick sample it helped to reduce heat accumulation and for complex trajectory cut it reduced the beam scanning speed to suit the limitations of galvanometer scanners. The relative cutting speeds for different samples were 0.4 mm2/s (Fig. 10 (a)), 1.4 mm2/s (Fig. 10(d)) and 2.9 mm2/s (Fig. 10(f)). The relative cutting speed of an oblique cylinder was calculated using the slant height (Fig. 10(f)) instead of thickness. 4. Summary and conclusions In this work, we have demonstrated the efficient and fast cutting of thick soda-lime glass using the GHz burst regime. The main results of this work are written below. • To achieve the highest cutting speed, the pulse duration must be chosen considering the maximum laser burst energy. For larger burst energies, higher pitch and higher number of pulses per burst with longer pulse duration are favorable. • For 4.8 mm-thick glass, the highest cutting speed of 4.2 mm/s and linear cutting efficiency of 0.13 mm/J were achieved with pico- second pulses in GHz bursts and optimized pitch. • To the best of our knowledge, we have shown the highest relative cutting speed and relative cutting efficiency compared to other studies on laser subtractive cutting of glass. • The average chippings for the investigated processing parameters were 90 µm and 400 µm for the bottom and top surfaces, respec- tively. The average sidewall roughness (Ra) was 2.3 µm. • Cutting of glass with various thickness ranging from 1 mm to 18.8 mm, an oblique cylinder with a 35-degree slant angle, and complex contour cutting is demonstrated showing extensive cutting capabilities. Overall, GHz burst laser can offer significant cutting process throughput increase compared to the classical ultrashort pulsed laser ablation. However, high-speed cutting regime comes with an inferior cutting quality. Nevertheless, modern laser control allows quick shifting Fig. 8. Scheme of a relative cutting speed. M.
