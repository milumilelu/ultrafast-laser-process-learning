By establishing a synergistic coupling framework that integrates the two- temperature model (TTM) and the hydrodynamic model (HD), this study elucidates the inter­ action mechanisms between femtosecond lasers and materials: high laser power (18–24 W) and ultrashort pulse width (300 fs) can significantly elevate electron peak temperature and accelerate material removal, and a low repetition frequency (200 kHz) enhances single-pulse energy to optimize machining efficiency. The primary removal mechanisms for the material are evapora­ tion of metal vapor and splash removal driven by recoil pressure. After 4000 ps, the lateral liquid flow velocity in the molten pool exceeds the evaporation rate, leading to the formation of a stacking area. Introduction Turbine blades serve as critical hot-section components of aeroengines, and the machining of film-cooling holes has emerged as an essential approach to enhance blade cooling performance and improve high-temperature resistance. The film-cooling technique [1] introduces cooling airflow through precisely engineered holes forming a protective gas film that insulates blades from hot combustion gases, thereby extending their thermal service life. The geometric accuracy and quality of these cooling holes directly determine blade reliability and engine operational safety [2], imposing stringent challenges on manufacturing processes. Currently, laser-electrical spark composite manufacturing, ultrasonic-electrical spark composite manufacturing, and electrohy­ draulic beam-electrochemical composite machining are widely adopted for manufacturing film-cooling holes in superalloy turbine blades [3]. However, these approaches have drawbacks such as complex processing procedures, low machining accuracy, and limited production efficiency. This study focuses on the Inconel 718 nickel‑based superalloy, which is widely used in aero‑turbine engine blades. An innovative coupled TTM‑HD model for femtosecond laser‑metal interaction was developed, and numerical simulations were carried out based on the COMSOL software platform. The TTM quantitatively characterizes the evolution of the electron-lattice temperature field, revealing the effects of laser parameters on energy deposition. The HD dynamically tracks solid-liquid-vapor phase transitions and melt pool dynamics. Through thermal source and temperature field data exchange, the coupled models achieve a comprehensive simulation spanning from electron excitation to material removal. The model’s accuracy is validated through single-pulse tests, providing theoretical guidance for fabricating high-quality film-cooling holes. 2. Multi-physics modeling framework When a femtosecond laser irradiates a metal surface, electrons reach their peak temperature within femtosecond timescales through laser energy absorption. This energy is then transferred to the lattice via electron-phonon coupling, inducing lattice tem­ perature rise that ultimately leads to thermal ablation. In the ablation of the Inconel 718 superalloy under intense femtosecond laser irradiation, the high-energy-density beam focuses onto the surface, generating localized high-temperature, high-pressure plasma. The lattice structure inside Inconel 718 superalloy is affected by the strong pressure impulse caused by plasma expansion, as well as the rapid deposition and dissipation of energy, which leads to the phase explosion phenomenon, and the recoil pressure pushes the material to spray outwards perpendicular to the surface of the target. Plasma plumes and shockwaves are formed, which ablate and remove materials from the surface of the Inconel 718 superalloy [26]. Therefore, a coupled two-temperature-hydrodynamic (TTM-HD) model was developed to investigate the femtosecond laser interactions with Inconel 718. 2.1. Two-temperature model (TTM) 2.1.1. As an intrinsic physical property, lattice heat capacity remains invariant under variations in laser intensity. Assuming negligible solid solution effects, the lattice heat capacity of Inconel 718 superalloy can be approximated as a linear superposition of its principal constituent elements. Hydrodynamic model (HD) When establishing a hydrodynamic model for femtosecond laser single-pulse ablation of Inconel 718 superalloy, certain as­ sumptions must be made to simplify the model: (1) The laser energy is applied to the liquid-gas interface as a thermal source term. (2) Energy dissipation through laser absorption in the ionized vapor plume is neglected. (3) The material is assumed to be isotropic to simplify the nonlinear computations. (4) The high-temperature molten liquid in the melt pool and metal vapor are treated as incompressible Newtonian fluids under laminar flow conditions. (5) The interference between the surface-reflected and incident laser beams is neglected. The interaction between femtosecond lasers and materials constitutes a highly complex multi-physics process, involving phase transition phenomena (melting and vaporization) coupled with the dynamic evolution of solid-liquid-vapor tri-phase systems [36]. The thermophysical properties of the material exhibit spatial variation with position and temporal evolution with phase state, with solid-liquid-vapor tri-phase coexistence. Therefore, this study employs the enthalpy-porosity method [37] to simulate the solid-to-liquid phase transition, and adopts the level-set approach [38] to model the liquid metal evaporation process. Coupling and validation of the two-temperature and hydrodynamic models The TTM was employed to analyze the thermal evolution within the electron-lattice system under single-pulse irradiation, examining both the influence of laser processing parameters and the depth-dependent variations in electron and lattice temperatures. The HD was employed to investigate temperature and velocity fields within the melt pool and metallic vapor plume during single-pulse femtosecond laser irradiation. Numerical simulations based on the TTM enable the acquisition of dynamic evolution curves of lattice temperature at the material surface versus time. By incorporating an electron overestimation temperature correction [43–45], the accuracy of temperature field calculations is significantly enhanced. These calibrated temperature-time data provide critical thermal source conditions for the hydrodynamic model, enabling effective data coupling between the TTM and HD. Based on the TTM sim­ ulations, this study identified a favorable range of process parameters for femtosecond laser drilling. Therefore, laser powers of 18 W and 24 W (200 kHz repetition frequency and 300 fs pulse width) were selected to establish the HD model. As shown in Fig. 3, the surface temperature profiles obtained from both models exhibit consistent trends within the first 20 ps, confirming that this thermal source configuration accurately describes the effects of 18 W and 24 W laser irradiation. 3. Fig. 5 presents the electron-lattice coupling times and equilibrium temperatures at different locations. Upon laser irradiation, the surface electron temperature rapidly peaks at approximately one ps, while the lattice temperature remains comparatively low but exhibits continuous increase during this timeframe. Following laser termination, energy transfer from electrons to the lattice via electron-phonon coupling induces rapid electron cooling and accelerates lattice heating to the phase separation threshold, initiating ablation. The system ultimately achieves thermal equilibrium, with synchronous temperature decay in both subsystems.
