Chen Keywords: Nickel-based superalloy Film-cooling hole Thermal barrier coating Creep property Oxidation Rafting A B S T R A C T In this study, creep tests were conducted on DD6 single crystal superalloy specimens containing different film cooling holes (FCHs) and thermal barrier coating (TBC) under conditions of 1000 ◦C and 300 MPa. Through the analysis of experimental data and characterization of the microstructure after creep fracture, the effects of FCHs and TBC on the high-temperature creep properties of DD6 alloy were evaluated. The results indicate that all specimens cracked from the FCH edges. During the creep process, oxide layer and recrystallization formed at the FCH surface, which facilitated the premature initiation of cracks. The γ′ precipitates near the FCHs exhibit varying degrees of rafting due to different stress states, the high-stress zone with more significant γ′ precipitate rafting near the FCHs provided a pathway for crack propagation. The interdiffusion of elements between the TBC and the substrate altered the microstructure of the alloy and reduced creep performance. These findings reveal the influence of FCHs and TBC on the creep properties of DD6 alloy and explain the mechanisms, providing practical insights for the assessment of creep performance of turbine blades. 1. Introduction Nickel-based single crystal superalloys are widely used in advanced turbine blades due to their excellent high-temperature creep properties and oxidation resistance. During service, the combination of thermal stress caused by internal cooling and centrifugal stress from high-speed rotation is considered the primary stress applied to turbine blades. Additionally, high temperatures significantly affect microstructural stability, and creep damage caused by the combined effect of stress and temperature is one of the main failure modes in nickel-based single crystal superalloy turbine blades [1]. High-temperature creep strength is regarded as a fundamental indicator of the mechanical performance of Ni-based single crystal superalloys [2]. In recent years, extensive research has been conducted on the high-temperature creep properties of nickel-based single crystal superalloys, particularly the typical γ/γ′ phase strengthening mechanism [3,4]. Under extreme conditions above 1000 ◦C, significant rafting and/or coarsening of the γ′ phase occurs in the alloy, leading to the merging of cubic γ′ phases into plate-like structures, which further hinders dislocation movement and enhances creep performance [5–9]. With the increasing demands on the perfor­ mance of aircraft engines, raising the intake temperature has become crucial for achieving greater thrust [10]. Simply improving the creep properties of the turbine blade alloy is no longer sufficient to meet the challenges posed by the rising intake temperatures, making cooling technology one of the key research areas for enhancing blade performance. The introduction of film cooling holes (FCHs) has been proven to be an effective cooling method [11,12], reducing thermal stress through their incorporation into turbine blade surfaces. However, the dense distribution of FCHs compromises the blade’s structural integrity, inducing a multiaxial stress state that elevates surface defect suscepti­ bility and modifies the alloy’s creep fracture mechanism. Under operational conditions, most turbine blade failures initiate from surface defects adjacent to FCHs, with crack nucleation predominantly occurring in these regions [15]. Furthermore, the introduction of non-conventional FCHs exacerbates stress concentration, making stress concentration and localized plastic deformation more prone to crack propagation. The shear slip induced by localized stress concentration can lead to a com­ bination of cleavage and ductile fracture around the holes [16]. Studies have shown that the high-temperature creep life of structures with FCHs is generally lower than that of structures without FCHs [17]. Therefore, investigating the fracture mechanisms and microstructural evolution of nickel-based single crystal superalloys with FCHs under creep conditions has significant theoretical and engineering value. In recent years, re­ searchers have studied the effects of various characteristics of FCHs on the high-temperature creep properties of materials, with features such as the geometric shape, inclination angle, spatial distribution, and surface quality of the FCHs having a significant impact on the fracture and failure behavior of blades [18–21]. Additionally, the oxidation of the surface of the FCHs in high-temperature environments also has a notable impact on creep performance [22–24]. In addition to FCHs, thermal barrier coating (TBC) technology is also extensively employed on turbine blades to substantially enhance oper­ ational temperature capabilities, enabling operation at temperatures approaching 1500 ◦C [25]. TBCs typically consist of a three-layer structure, with the top layer being a ceramic layer made of yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) that exhibits exceptional thermal resis­ tance and an exceptionally high melting point [26–28]. Beneath the ceramic layer is a metallic bond coat, commonly composed of NiCrAlY or NiCoCrAlY alloys, which enhance corrosion resistance and adhesion of the coating through the addition of chromium and yttrium [29]. Significantly, the thermally grown oxide (TGO) layer developing during bond coat oxidation serves as a critical protective barrier under elevated temperature service conditions. Nevertheless, prolonged thermal expo­ sure renders TBC systems susceptible to degradation, primarily through accelerated TGO growth that induces localized stress concentrations and ultimately promotes coating spallation [30–36]. The precipitation of brittle topologically close-packed (TCP) phases beneath the bond coat is of particular concern, which not only initiates micro­ cracking but also depletes critical solid-solution strengthening elements from the substrate, thereby significantly deteriorating the alloy’s creep resistance [38–40]. Currently, the effects of FCHs on the creep properties of Ni-based single crystal superalloys have garnered widespread attention, but research on non-conventional FCHs such as dustpan film cooling hole remains relatively limited. In particular, the combined effects of the non- conventional FCHs and TBCs on the high-temperature creep perfor­ mance and fracture behavior of nickel-based single crystal superalloys are not clear, which hinders their application in the turbine blades in these high thrust-to-weight aeroengines. Therefore, high-temperature creep experiments were conducted on nickel-based single crystal su­ peralloy specimens with the various non-conventional FCH shapes and TBCs in this study, and the microstructure inside the specimens after the creep tests was characterized and analyzed, exploring the effects of the non-conventional FCHs and TBCs on the high-temperature creep prop­ erties and creep mechanisms of nickel-based single crystal superalloys. 2. Creep tests Creep tests for specimens with different FCHs were conducted ac­ cording to the GB/T2039-2012 standard, using a GBR-30J high-tem­ perature creep testing machine. The test temperature was 1000 ◦C, and the nominal applied stress was 300 MPa. The test temperature was monitored using thermocouples attached to the specimens. Once the temperature reached 1000 ◦C, it was held for 1 h before applying the load. The loading was applied using a mechanical lever system, and the creep deformation was measured outside the furnace using an exten­ someter connected to an extension rod. To ensure consistency and sta­ tistical significance of the test results, all specimens were tested on the same machine, with three specimens tested for each hole type. 2.3. The processed image has only two colors, where black is the γ′ phase and white is the γ phase. The average thickness of the γ phase within the region of interest was obtained using the software package ImageJ, specifically employing the local thickness plugin. The local thickness algorithm calculates the thickness of a structure by determining the maximum diameter of a sphere that can fit within the structure at each point, effectively measuring the local thickness. From the generated thickness map, the mean value was extracted to determine the average thickness of the γ phase in the specified region. After obtaining the average thickness of the γ phase, the images were then processed using the Skeletonize function in ImageJ to extract the skeletons of the γ and γ′ phases. To quantify the degree of rafting at different positions around the FCH, the dimensionless parameter R, as described by the Epishin method [42], was used for analysis. The value of R was calculated based on the skeletonized im­ ages. Specifically, the numbers of termination in the γ skeleton were counted as the value p, and the numbers of termination in the γ′ skeleton were counted as the value p’. The parameter R was then determined as the ratio of p to p’. Additionally, to understand the deformation mech­ anisms in different regions of the specimen during creep testing, TEM lamellae were extracted from the regions adjacent to the FCH and the TBC respectively and from the alloy matrix using focused ion beam, and then were examine in FEI Talos F200s TEM under two-beam condition. 3. The overall fracture morphology of the creep specimens with 45◦ cylindrical holes, 0◦expanded holes, and 90◦dustpan holes is shown in Fig. 6. All specimens fracture at the FCHs. Fig. 6 (a2, b2, c2) show the interface region between TBC and the substrate, where the multilayer structure of the coating can be observed. The top YSZ layer has a columnar crystal structure that is perpendicular to the surface of the specimens, with a thickness of about 200 μm. After high-temperature creep fracture, vertical cracks can be observed between the YSZ columnar grains at the fracture site, and part of the YSZ layer has visibly detached from the bond coat. The exposed bond coat in the detached areas shows significant oxidation, with many pores observed inside the bond coat, forming a porous oxide structure. A relatively smooth instantaneous fracture zone can be found near the surface of the spec­ imen, far from the FCHs, on the fracture surface. Apart from the instantaneous fracture zone, the crack propagation zone occupies most of the fracture area. Based on the location and shape of the instanta­ neous fracture zone, along with the wavy steps observed in the crack propagation zone, the crack initiation region can be identified. For the cylindrical and expanded hole specimens, the crack initiation region is mainly located at the sharp intersection between the FCH outlet and the TBC.
