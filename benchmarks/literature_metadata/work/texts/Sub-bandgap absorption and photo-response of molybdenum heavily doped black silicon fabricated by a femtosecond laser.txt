ID 425803); published 1 July 2021 Molybdenum (Mo)-doped black silicon (Si) is obtained by using femtosecond laser irradiation. The concentration of Mo atoms at the depth from 10 to 200 nm has exceeded 1019 cm−3. In contrast, the carrier concentration in the Mo-doped layer is lower than 1015 cm−3. The surface mor- phologies with ripple and conical spike microstructures are formed by changing the pulsed laser ﬂuences. The Mo- doped Si samples exhibit a sub-bandgap (1100∼2500 nm) absorptance of more than 60% at a wavelength of 1310 nm. A Mo-doped Si photodetector is made, and the responsiv- ity of the device for 1310 nm is up to 76 mA/W at a −10 V bias. © 2021 Optical Society of America https://doi.org/10.1364/OL.425803 As one of the most investigated semiconductors, silicon (Si) has been widely used in integrated circuits, photodetection, optical waveguide integration, etc. In the past half-century, microelectronics revolution has made great advancements and promoted the rapid development of information tech- nology. Owing to the huge industrial capabilities of Si-based microelectronics technology and mature complementary metal- oxide-semiconductor transistor, Si photonics technology has also been development rapidly and has been used for optical communication and optical interconnection [1–4]. As one of the key components of all-Si optic electronics integrated circuit chips in the future, the research on Si photodetectors has become particularly important. Although, limited by its own bandgap, the crystalline Si cannot detect the infrared light beyond its absorption edge, the sulfur (S)-hyperdoped “black Si” obtained by femtosecond (fs) laser pulses irradia- tion can break this limit [5]. The S-hyperdoped black Si can extend the absorption edge of crystalline Si to the short-wave infrared region, and its absorptance has exceeded 90% within the wavelengths of 250 ∼2500 nm [6]. Besides, the selenium- hyperdoped and tellurium-hyperdoped black Si also have high infrared absorption [7]. Based on the enhanced infrared absorp- tion, the chalcogen-hyperdoped black Si photodetectors have been obtained [8–12]. Recently, in order to suppress the dark current of a black Si photodetector, some methods have been adopted, such as rapid thermal annealing, hydrogenated surface passivation, designing lateral heterojunction, and elevating the substrate temperature [13–16]. However, there is high free carrier concentration (1019 ∼1020 cm−3) in the S-hyperdoped black Si owing to a large electroactivity of S impurities in Si. The free carrier absorption does not contribute to the infrared pho- toelectric conversion process [8,17]. Compared with chalcogen impurities [7,9,18], transition metal is an ideal doping element to prepare of black Si for reducing the free carrier concentration [19–21]. As a stable transition metal, molybdenum (Mo) shows a low electroactivity in Si, and a typical Mo energy level (donor) is located 0.34 eV above the valence band maximum (VBM) of Si [22]. Therefore, the Mo impurity is expected to prepare black Si for improving sub-bandgap absorption and decreasing free carrier concentration. In this Letter, Mo as a dopant has been used to fabricate heavily Mo-doped black Si. At last, the Mo-doped black Si photodetectors based on double-junction have been proposed. The device can work under both forward bias and reverse bias. The Mo-doped Si photodiode (PD) has a sub-bandgap photo-responsivity of 76 mA/W for 1310 nm. The novel electronic and optical properties of Mo-hyperdoped black Si make Si very competitive for processing infrared photodetectors. The Mo precursor dopant was prepared. N-Si (111) wafers (resistivity >1500 ·cm, 250 µm) were cleaned by standard cleaning procedures [23]. After that, high purity (99.95%) Mo ﬁlm of 40 nm was deposited onto the cleaned Si wafer by a radio-frequency (13.56 MHz) magnetron sputtering apparatus [21]. For the fs laser irradiation process, the Mo-covered Si substrates were put into a vacuum cavity ﬁlled with Argon (Ar) of 0.1 MPa, which was ﬁxed onto a two-dimensional translation stage. The fs laser spot of 150 µm in diameter was focused onto the Mo-covered Si sur- face by a focal lens. In general, the changes in microstructure size with laser ﬂuence are determined by laser- induced periodic surface structures. After fs laser irradiation, the interference between the incident light and the light scattered by minor defects on the surface results in inhomogeneous energy deposition. Ablation and melt formation occur at non-uniform depths, creating capillary waves. Rapid re-solidiﬁcation sub- sequently freezes the ripple structure in place. For the lower laser ﬂuences (F ) of 0.28 J/cm2, ripple structures with a period of 0.5 ∼0.7 µm are formed. Next, the ripple beads can concentrate a laser into the valleys between the beads. As a result, incident laser pulses prefer- entially remove material to the sides of the beads, leaving the conical structures behind. Thus, when the laser ﬂuence increases to 0.71 J/cm2, the surface morphology changes into coni- cal spikes (height of 4 ∼5 µm). Then the material removal becomes more obvious, and the height of the conical spikes grows with an increase of the laser ﬂuences. The long axis of the microstructure is perpendicular to the polarization of the laser beam, but independent of the substrate’s crystallographic orientation [24,25]. The sub-bandgap absorptance (1100 ∼2500 nm) is determined by the heavy doping of Mo atoms [21] and ablation-induced structural defects [26]. Next, the absorptance of the annealed samples was shown in Fig. 3(b), and the annealing process can reduce the sub-bandgap absorption of the samples. Figure 3(c) calculates the deference (1A = Aunannealed −Aannealed) in sub-bandgap absorptance between the unannealed (Aunannealed) and annealed (Aannealed) samples at 823 K. After thermal treatment, the absorptance evidently decreases for the sample fabricated at the laser ﬂuence of 1.84 J/cm2 due to a large number of amorphous phases and structural defect densities [see Fig. 4(a)]; however, the absorp- tance slightly decreases for the other samples with a change within 8%, which do not show the evident change rule with the laser ﬂuence. In addition, annealing mostly affects the sub- bandgap absorptance in the region of 1.1 ∼1.8 µm, which is related to structural defects (Urbach states) [28]. The detailed explanation for variation in the absorptance before and after annealing will be discussed in the section on Raman spectra. Furthermore, the dependence of absorptance on annealing temperatures is investigated. Figure 3(d) shows the absorp- tance of the unannealed and annealed Mo-doped Si samples (F = 0.71 J/cm2) at different temperatures (823 ∼1223 K). In comparison to the sub-bandgap annealed at 823 K, the change of sub-bandgap absorptance is not obvious for a higher anneal- ing temperature. The inset of Fig. 3(d) shows the variation of the absorptance at a wavelength of 1310 nm after annealing at different temperatures. After annealing, the changes in absorp- tance do not exceed 8%, indicating that the ablation-induced structural defects nearly disappeared after annealing at a higher temperature than 1023K. Then the Raman spectra of Mo-doped black Si were mea- sured. From Fig. 4(a), all the Si samples display the 520 cm−1 (P1) vibration peak of crystalline Si (Si-I). For the Mo-doped black Si samples (F ≥0.71 J/cm2), other new vibration peaks (P2: 300 cm−1; P3: 504 ∼509 cm−1) appear in the Raman spectra, which are related to the ablation-induced amorphous Si (α-Si) phase produced by the melting and re-solidiﬁcation process [29]. With an increase of the laser ﬂuence, the full width at half-maximum centered at P3 vibration peak changes from 20 to 35 cm−1; meanwhile, the position of P3 shifts from 509 to 504 cm−1. The redshift observed for the P3 vibration peak with increasing laser ﬂuence may be driven by stress in the laser-irradiated layer, which is determined by ablation strength. Fig. 5. In addition, the relative peak intensity I3/I1 (I1: the intensity of P1; I3: the intensity of P3) markedly increases with an increase of the laser ﬂuence, which indicates that the stronger laser irradiation will cause more serious damage in the Mo-doped Si layer. Thus, the higher defect density in the α-Si phase can evidently affect the sub-bandgap absorption of Mo-doped black Si, as mentioned above in Fig. 3(a). Further, Fig. 4(b) shows the Raman spectra of annealed Mo-doped black Si at different temperatures. Compared with the unannealed sample, the relative intensity I3/I1 gradually decreases with an increase of annealing temperature (823 ∼1123 K) until the vibration peak P3 disappears at the temperature of 1223 K. After annealing, the reduction in sub-bandgap absorption of Mo-doped Si in Fig. 3(d) is related to the decrease in intensity of P3 vibration peak in Fig. 4(b), which means that the laser-induced α-Si phase in the black Si layer can contribute to the sub-bandgap absorption, and this part of the absorption is thermally sensitive becauseoftheunstableα-Siphase. Atlast,fromtheelectricalnature,theMo-dopedblackSilayer annealed at 823 K shows N-type conduction type with a sheet electronic density of 9.3 × 109 cm−2. Taking into account the depth of the doping layer of 100 nm ∼1 µm (see Fig. 2), the carrier concentration of Mo atoms in the black Si layer is below 1015 cm−3. Then black Si PDs (5 mm × 5 mm) are fabricated, andtheschematicdiagramofthedeviceisshowninFig.5. This device structure can be equivalent to two face-to-face Schottky PDs (Al-black Si PD, Au-Si PD). As in the I-Vcurves shown in Fig. 5, the Al-Si PD shows larger responsivity of 76 mA/W for 1310 nm at −10 V bias while the responsivity of the Au-Si PD is 1.4 mA/W under bias of 10 V. Affected by the crystalline quality and structural defects of the Mo-doped layer, the carrier mobility (401 cm2 V−1S−1) is below that of crys- talline Si. Thus, the photocurrent and responsivity of the device will be lower at a low bias. As shown in Fig. 5, the responsivity of the PD increases with increasing bias. To understand the work- ing mechanism of the device, the energy-band diagram has been given in Fig. 6. The EC j, EVj, and E F represent the conduction band minimum, VBM, and Fermi level of Si, respectively, where j = 1 (for the Mo-doped Si layer) or 2 (for the Si substrate). EFAl and EFAu represent the Fermi levels of metal Al and Au, respectively. For the reverse bias demonstrated in Fig. 5, the Al-Si Schottky PD is cut off in Fig. 6(a), and the electron-hole (e–h) pairs produced in the depletion region will eventually

In contrast, for the forward bias illustrated in Fig. 5, the Au-Si Schottky PD is cut off, and the photogenerated e–h pairs in the black Si layer within the diffusion length should pass though the black Si layer and Si substrate; then they are separated by the electric ﬁeld E 2. Comparing the two cases, we see that the Au-Si Schottky junction shows a lower photocurrent due to the recombination effect of photogenerated carriers in black Si layer. However, the Al-Si junction can achieve more effective photoelectric conversion for sub-bandgap light by reducing the chance of being recombinedintheblackSilayer. In summary, the heavily Mo-doped black Si samples have been prepared by fs laser irradiation, and the peak concentration of Mo atoms in the black Si layer is larger than 1019 cm−3. The ripple structures and conical spikes are created. The Mo-doped black Si samples perform high absorption in the region of NIR wavelengths because of the heavy doping of Mo impurities, laser ablation-induced detects, and textured surface. The ther- mal stability and mechanism of sub-bandgap absorption has been discussed combining the Raman spectra analysis. The Mo-doped Si PD worked at the NIR region is made based on the enhanced sub-bandgap absorption. At a bias of −10 V, the black Si Schottky PD exhibits a responsivity of 76 mA/W for 1310 nm light. The study on PDs in this Letter proves poten- tial applications in infrared detection of Mo-doped black Si materials. Funding. National Natural Science Foundation of China (61775077). Disclosures.
