Introduction Nickel based superalloy components such as blades and noz- zle guide vanes are used in hot sections of advanced gas turbine engines. During use, these components are provided protection by air cooling through internal cooling channels. Additional pro- tection is provided by applying a thermal barrier coating (TBC) on the component surface. While the outer 7% yttria-stabilized zirconia (7YSZ) ceramic layer of these multi-layer coatings pro- vides thermal insulation, the inner metallic Pt–aluminide bond coat protects the component from the oxidation damage. The internal cooling channels are connected to the surface of the component through a large number of cooling holes. These cooling holes are typically 0.3–1 mm in diameter and are machined through wall thickness ranging from 0.5 mm to 4 mm (McNally et al., 2004). Since fabrication of the cooling holes requires machining through an electrically non-conducting 7YSZ layer, traditional methods such as electrochemical and electro-discharge machining are usually found unsuitable for the purpose. Laser machining, on the other hand, is a non-contact method involving material removal through melt-ejection/vaporization by a high power laser beam that is focused on the component surface. Machining of superalloy components using Nd-YAG type nanosecond (ns) pulsed lasers is a fairly well established practice in the aviation industry (McNally et al., 2004; Yeo et al., 1994). However, because of the longer pulse widths associated with these lasers, which are typically several nanoseconds, a signiﬁcant amount of thermal energy deposited during the laser-material interaction diffuses to the surrounding material during machining (Chichkov et al., 1996). As a consequence, collateral damages con- sisting of recast layer formation, melt debris, and micro-cracking often accompany ns laser machining (McNally et al., 2004; Yeo et al., 1994; Bandyopadhyay et al., 2002). In case of machining of thermal barrier coated components, additional damage in the form of melting, cracking and delamination of the YSZ layer may also be introduced (McNally et al., 2004; Forge et al., 1988; Voisey and Clyne, 2004; Corcoran et al., 2002; Kamalu et al., 2002; Low et al., 2003). Such damage to the YSZ layer is undesirable because it can potentially cause accelerated coating spallation around the hole under cyclic heating and cooling during operation. The advent of chirped-pulse ampliﬁcation (CPA) technology during the past decade has led to the commercial availability of powerful and reliable femtosecond (fs) pulsed lasers. Due to the ultra-short duration of fs pulses, which is typically of the order of 100 fs (1 fs =10−15 s), these lasers allow high quality machining and patterning of solids to be performed without any visible ther- mal/mechanical damage of the types mentioned earlier (Krüger and 0924-0136/$ – see front matter © 2009 Elsevier B.V. In the present paper, detailed characterization of fs laser machined holes generated under various processing conditions on thermal barrier coated CMSX4 superalloy has been conducted. Although the positive attributes of fs laser machining have been highlighted, the primary objective of this study has been to examine the important features of the generated holes such as the shape, size and the sur- face ﬁnish of the hole wall. The issue of collateral damage, if any, associated with fs laser machining has also been examined. Further, the effect of the presence of the holes on the thermal cycling life of the TBC has also been investigated. 2. Experimental details The thermal barrier coated samples used in this study were provided by Howmet Research Corp., U.S.A. The substrate for the samples was the Ni-base single crystal superalloy CMSX4 with the nominal composition (wt.%) of Ni–5.6 Al–1.0 Ti–6.5 Ta–6.5 Cr–0.6 Mo–6.0 W–9.0 Co–3.0 Re–0.1 Hf. The thermal barrier coating consisted of a MDC 150L Pt–aluminide bond coat and an elec- tron beam physical vapor deposited (EB-PVD) 7YSZ ceramic layer. The thickness of the 7YSZ layer was either 125 m (5 mil) or 225 m (9 mil), while that of the bond coat was approximately 60 m. A commercially available Ti:sapphire fs pulsed laser based on CPA technology (Clark MXR CPA2001) was used for machin- ing through-thickness holes in coated samples of three different thicknesses, namely, 0.4 mm, 0.8 mm and 1.5 mm. The wavelength, the pulse duration and the pulse repetition rate for the laser were 780 nm, 150 fs and 1 kHz, respectively. The laser pulses had a Gaus- sian spatial energy distribution and the laser power was varied by introducing ﬁlters of various optical densities in the beam path. The laser beam was focused onto the sample surface using a plano-convex BK-7 glass lens of focal length 350 mm and the sample surface was maintained normal to the laser beam. Holes were machined from the YSZ coating side and fabricated with a trepanning technique in which the coated sample was moved in a circular motion (or rotation) repetitively with respect to the stationary laser beam, as schematically shown in Fig. 1(a). The velocity maintained for the circular motion was 0.1 mm s−1. Holes of two diameters, namely 600 m and 300 m, were machined on each sample. The number of such trepanning rotations needed for completing a hole depended on the thickness of the sample as well as the laser power used, which was maintained in the range 290–540 mW. A few holes were also made by percussion drilling method, i.e. by pulsing the laser repetitively at a given location on the YSZ coating surface until a through hole in the sample was achieved. For estimating the surface ﬁnish on the hole wall, through- thickness straight-line cuts were made on the samples at similar ﬂuence levels used for generating the holes. The schematic of a straight-line cut is shown in Fig. 1(b). Subsequently, the average roughness, Ra, for the cut surface was measured by using a Dektak3 precision proﬁlometer. The vertical resolution of the proﬁlome- ter was 1 nm. The measurement was made electromechanically by moving the specimen surface beneath a diamond-tipped stylus using a high-precision stage. For each measurement, the dis- tance scanned by the stylus on the sample surface was 2 mm. Each Ra value presented in this paper is an average of ﬁve measurements. Fig. 2. Thermal cycling tests of the machined samples were conducted to evaluate the damage evolution in the vicinity of the machined holes. For the thermal cycling tests, samples with approximate dimensions of 5 mm × 10 mm were prepared for all the three sam- ple thicknesses. Subsequently, four holes, two each with diameters of 600 m and 300 m, were machined on all the samples. Two samples with the machined holes were tested for each sample thickness. Thermal cycling was conducted at 1100 ◦C in air using an automated cyclic furnace. Each one-hour cycle consisted of 10 min heat-up, 45 min dwell (at 1100 ◦C) and 5 min forced cooling by a fan at room temperature. The test was carried out for a maximum of 1000 cycles, which was based on the reported life of 900–1400 one-hour cycles for the 7YSZ thermal barrier coatings (Sridharan et al., 2005, Wen et al., 2006). The material structure in the vicinity of the machined holes was observed both on the YSZ side and on the metal side using a Hitachi S3200N scanning electron microscope (SEM) operating at 15–20 kV. The hole diameters were measured from the SEM micrographs. For acquiring the cross-sectional views of the holes, the samples were polished using the standard metallographic procedures. Subse- quently, the polished surface was etched with a solution consisting of 33% acetic acid, 33% nitric acid, 1% hydroﬂuoric acid and 33% dis- tilled water (all in vol.%), to reveal the microstructural features of the substrate alloy and the bond coat. 3. Results and discussion The spot radius, ωo, of the Gaussian pulses used in the present case was 27 m, as has been reported in a previous study (Das et al., 2008). Using this ωo, the power range of 290–540 mW used for machining can be converted into the ﬂuence range of 25–47 J cm−2 (Mannion et al., 2003). This ﬂuence level is 16–29 times of the single-pulse threshold ﬂuence of 1.6 J cm−2 for 7YSZ (Feng et al., 2006; Das et al., 2008), and 83–157 times the threshold ﬂuence of 0.3 J cm−2 for the CMSX4 superalloy (Ma et al., 2007). Most of the previously reported studies on hole drilling involving both ns and fs lasers have considered percussion drilling (McNally et al., 2004; Yeo et al., 1994; Voisey and Clyne, 2004; Feng et al., 2006). However, machining with percussion technique, where the test piece is held stationary with respect to the laser beam, has the limitation of generating holes only up to a certain maximum diameter, as dictated by the spot size of the beam. Fig. 3 presents the plan view of a percussion-machined hole obtained using 50,000 pulses at 25 J cm−2 on the 0.4 mm thick sample. The diameter of this hole was approximately 120 m which was about the maxi- mum achievable diameter through percussion technique. However, as previously mentioned, much larger cooling holes, typically in the range 300–1000 m, are required on TBC coated components (McNally et al., 2004). This necessitates the adoption of trepanning technique for machining, as used in this study. In the following discussion, the YSZ side of the sample, on which machining was initiated, has been referred to the entry side and the opposite metal side as the exit side (see Fig. 1(a)). 3.1. Shape, size and surface ﬁnish of holes Fig. 4(a–d) shows the plan views of the holes with an intended diameter of 600 m, on both entry and exit sides for 0.4 mm and 1.5 mm thick samples. The circularity of the holes on the entry Fig. 5. Schematic showing taper, , in a laser machined hole. side is clearly evident. On the exit side, however, the holes were somewhat less circular. Table 1 lists the dimensions and other rele- vant experimental details of the holes obtained on various samples. Because of the Gaussian distribution of the laser pulses, a certain degree of taper was found with the machined holes, with the entry diameter, Den, being appreciably larger than the intended diame- ter. For instance, for the aimed diameters of 600 m and 300 m, the Den values were approximately 800 m and 450 m, respec- tively (see Table 1). The exit diameter, Dex, however, remained close to the intended diameter. The degree of taper is represented in terms of the half-included angle, , of the cone formed by the hole (Bandyopadhyay et al., 2002), as shown in Fig. 5. This angle is given by the formula,  = tan−1((Den −Dex)/2h), where h is the thickness of the sample. For a cylindrical hole, where there is no taper present, the value of  equals zero. For the present holes, the taper  was fairly small and varied within a range of 3–6◦(see Table 1). It may be noted from Table 1 that the average depth generated per rotation during machining decreased with increase in the sample thickness, despite using increasingly higher ﬂuence. This can be attributed to the increasingly higher loss of laser energy due to scattering and absorption by the hole wall as the sample thickness increased (Bonse et al., 2001). For comparison, the Ra value for the as-coated YSZ surface was approximately 1.5 m (Das et al., 2008). Thus, it can be concluded that a fairly good surface ﬁnish of about 1.8 m, comparable to that for the as-received 7YSZ coating, was achieved for the fs machined holes. Usually, the initial surface generated during fs laser machining is fairly rough, but smoothens signiﬁcantly as the machining continues (Kamlage et al., 2003). Fig. 7(a) shows the cut surface obtained in case of a 100 m thick CMSX4 foil, where the through-thickness cut was achieved by a single laser scan at 36 J cm−2. The cut surface, as shown in Fig. 7(a), was quite rough and had a Ra value of 6.0 ± 0.6 m. The surface roughness signiﬁcantly decreased in subsequent laser scans of this surface. This is evident from Fig. 7(b) which shows the cut surface obtained in the same foil after six scans at 36 J cm−2. The rough- ness for this surface (Fig. 7(b)) was much lower at 1.9 ± 0.6 m. Once the through-thickness cut was achieved in the ﬁrst scan, the central high intensity region of the Gaussian laser pulses passed directly through the cut without absorption during the ﬁve subse- quent scans. However, the peripheral low ﬂuence region continued to interact with the cut surface causing mild material ablation, which led to the above enhancement in the surface ﬁnish. Such low ﬂuence material ablation during machining is the primary rea- son for which fs laser machining usually yields a superior surface ﬁnish (Kamlage et al., 2003). Fig. 8. Schematic of a Gaussian laser pulse showing the three regions, namely 1, 2 and 3, involved in the machining process. The above explanation can be extended to the present case of machining of the holes. While the central high intensity region of the laser pulses was primarily involved in increasing the depth through rapid material ablation, the low ﬂuence peripheral region caused smoothening of the already generated hole wall during suc- cessive rotations. The above-mentioned regions of a laser pulse involved in increasing the depth and enhancing the surface ﬁnish have been schematically shown as regions “1” and “2” in Fig. 8. Expectedly, the entry side of the hole experienced the maximum number of “smoothening” rotations and the exit side experienced the least number of such rotations. Reduced smoothening on the exit side was reﬂected in the form of circumferential waviness and reduced circularity, as seen in Figs. 4(c) and (d). This effect was more pronounced for the 1.5 mm thick sample because of its high thick- ness. As expected, the circularity of the holes on the exit side was greatly improved by allowing additional rotations beyond the num- ber needed for achieving a hole. This is evident from Fig. 9(a) and (b), which show the exit sides of the 600 m holes generated on 0.4 and 1.5 mm thick samples using 24 rotations at 25 J cm−2 and 180 rota- tions at 47 J cm−2, respectively. Collateral damage It has been emphasized in a number of previous studies that collateral material damage such as the heat affected zone, recast layer, spatter and micro-cracking, that are typically associated with ns laser machining, is mostly absent when a fs laser is used for machining (Krüger and Kautek, 1999; Kautek and Kruger, 1994; Nolte et al., 1997). To examine this aspect over a wider spectrum of processing conditions, the YSZ surface on the entry side of the hole and the hole cross-section were observed in the SEM. Fig. 10 presents the typical magniﬁed view of the YSZ surface close to a hole. It is clear that the YSZ columns adjoining the edge of the hole have suffered some degree of material loss, as evident from the blunting of the column tips.
