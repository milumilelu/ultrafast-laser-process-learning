This study presents a novel architecture integrating spherical thin-walled hollow shell (STHS)-structured fully stabilized tetragonal prime YSZ (t ′ -YSZ) coatings deposited via atmospheric plasma spraying onto NiCrAlY bond coats applied to René N5 substrates. High-fidelity femtosecond laser processing successfully produced defect-free cooling holes at inclination angles of 0◦, 30◦, and 60◦, entirely avoiding recast layer formation and heat-affected zones typical of conventional drilling techniques. After 1617 thermal shock cycles (1200◦C ⇄ambient), microstructural analysis demonstrated exceptional phase stability, with Raman spectroscopy detecting no monoclinic content ( < 0.5 vol%). The drilled holes maintained their geometry with crack propagation limited to below 2 µm. Cross-sectional elemental mapping further confirmed uniform composition in the substrate alloy, showing minimal deviation ( < 3.5 at.%) in critical strengthening elements (Al, Ta, Cr), thereby ensuring retained mechanical strength and oxidation barrier performance. The combined approach of STHS-engineered t ′ -YSZ microarchitecture and ultrafast laser machining establishes a robust framework for developing durable thermal management systems in next-generation turbine components subjected to cyclic thermal loads. 1 Introduction The pursuit of higher turbine inlet temperatures ( > 1700◦C) in advanced propulsion systems, driven by the technological evolution of aero-engines, imposes unprecedented thermal and mechanical loads exceeding the melting limits of nickel-based superalloys (typically ∼ 1150◦C) by approximately 550◦C [ 1, 2 ]. To address this thermal management challenge, conventional thermal management strategies that combine yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) with film-cooling technology demonstrate revolutionary potential but face funda- mental limitations [ 3, 4 ]. Standard 4 mol% YSZ TBCs, typically 200–300 µm thick, pro- vide significant passive thermal insulation ( ∼ 200◦C), largely attributed to phonon scattering at oxygen vacancies [ 2 ]. Mean- while, film cooling systems actively reduce substrate tempera- tures by ∼ 400◦C via high-pressure cooling air passing through microscale discrete hole arrays to form protective gas films [ 1 ]. Together, this integrated approach cumulatively elevates the substrate temperature endurance limit by about 600◦C [ 1 ]. How- ever, conventional YSZ undergoes a deleterious tetragonal-to- monoclinic (t→m) phase transformation above 1200◦C, accompa- nied by 3%–5% volumetric expansion, which can initiate microc- racks [ 5–7 ], which is exacerbated by laser drilling processes [ 8 ]. © 2026 The American Ceramic Society. Further limitations stem from conventional film-cooling hole (FCH) fabrication methodologies. The predominant “drill-then- coat” sequence relies on mechanical or thermal drilling (e.g., electrical discharge machining, EDM) prior to coating depo- sition, causing material infiltration into predrilled holes and pinhole occlusion, which degrades cooling efficiency and requires complex secondary machining [ 9 ]. Mechanical drilling induces edge coating spallation via stress concentration, while thermal drilling generates recast layers and heat-affected zones (HAZs), accelerating coating failure under thermal cycling [ 10–13 ]. To overcome these drawbacks, the “coat-then-drill” methodology has gained traction, with ultrafast laser drilling emerging as a critical enabler due to its ability to process ceramic TBCs and metallic substrates simultaneously [ 14 ]. Femtosecond laser drilling, characterized by pulse durations < 1 ps, minimizes thermal diffusion via ultra-high peak power, achieving zero substrate damage, no remelting, and suppressed cracks/HAZs— advantages over conventional thermal processing [ 10, 13, 15–18 ]. Recent studies, such as Feng and Shen’s work on femtosecond laser machining of TBC-coated superalloys, have validated its potential to reduce recast layers and microcracking, and it has been successfully applied to aero-engine metal components for high-quality FCH fabrication [ 8 ]. Nevertheless, femtosecond laser drilling imposes stringent demands on TBC phase stability, as the localized high temperature (exceeding 1423 K, the t→m transformation threshold) inherently affects YSZ microstructural integrity [ 5, 19 ]. Even with ultrafast pulses, YSZ’s low thermal conductivity causes heat accumulation during multipulse drilling [ 20 ], triggering phase transformation in conventional partially stabilized YSZ (which contains trace monoclinic phases). As documented by Feng and Shen [ 8 ], t→m transformation in air environments is driven by temperature elevation, accompanied by volume expansion and transformation stresses that provoke microcracking, hole-edge damage, and ultimately coating spallation. Recent advances have shown that coatings deposited from spher- ical thin-walled hollow-structured (STHS) t’-YSZ powders may address this bottleneck [ 21, 22 ]. Unlike conventional partially stabilized YSZ that suffers from severe Y-segregation and sub- sequent tetragonal-to-monoclinic (t→m) phase transformation, STHS t’-YSZ features an intrinsically fully stabilized nontrans- formable t’ phase with homogeneous yttrium distribution at the atomic level. This unique nanoarchitected material exhibits exceptional phase stability and outstanding sintering resistance at elevated temperatures, effectively suppressing the detrimen- tal t→m transformation and the associated 3%–5% volume expansion that generates excessive internal stress and induces coating cracking and delamination. Furthermore, the closed- pore microstructure derived from the STHS powder impedes grain-boundary diffusion of stabilizer elements, further retarding phase decomposition under long-term high-temperature service. Benefiting from these inherent advantages, its structural stabil- ity under laser-induced thermal shocks can eliminate phase- transformation-driven defects, even without auxiliary cooling environments. To validate this integrated solution, this study synergistically combines the intrinsic material advantages of STHS t’-YSZ with femtosecond laser ablation for fabricating high-integrity FCHs. STHS t’-YSZ coatings were deposited via atmospheric plasma spraying (APS) onto René N5 single-crystal superalloy substrates (consistent with turbine blade materials), followed by femtosecond laser drilling of FCHs at 0◦, 30◦, and 60◦inclination angles. Coated systems were subjected to thermal shock cycling, and post-thermal shock characterization was conducted using Raman spectroscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to evaluate hole-edge microstructural evolution, phase stability, and oxidation resistance. This work aims to demonstrate that fully stabilized STHS t’-YSZ maintains structural stability without t→m phase transformation during laser drilling, enabling defect-free FCHs with no edge chipping or spallation. The coating surface temperature was monitored using a microinfrared thermometer (Raytek MI3, Fluke Corporation, USA). To ensure statistical reliability, at least six samples for each coating configuration were tested throughout the thermal cycling experiment. Thermal shock tests were per- formed in accordance with ISO 14188:2012 [ 23 ], the international standard for TBC thermal cycle testing. Each thermal shock cycle consisted of two stages: heating from room temperature to 1200◦C over 100 s, holding at 1200◦C for 200 s, and then forced- air cooling back to room temperature within 50 s using an air compressor (flow rate: 2000 L/min, maximum pressure: 0.5 MPa). This sequence constituted one cycle, and a total of 1617 cycles were performed for each test. The flow rates of O2 and C3 H8 in the gas thermal shock test are shown in Table 2 . This predominantly “cold ablation” mechanism effectively suppresses HAZ formation, thereby preserving coating microstructural integrity and improving machining precision compared to conventional thermal drilling technologies (e.g., nanosecond laser or EDM). In conventional drilling processes, the interface between the ceramic coating and BC in the front-edge region of FCHs is highly prone to delamination. This phenomenon is driven by heat accumulation during prolonged energy–material interac- tion, with delamination length positively correlating with the intensification of interfacial thermal stress [ 13 ]. Such delamina- tion is directly attributed to reduced interfacial bonding strength induced by prolonged thermal exposure in traditional drilling techniques. In contrast, no interfacial delamination was observed in the t’-YSZ coatings prepared in this study, regardless of drilling angle. This exceptional interfacial stability stems from the synergistic effect of two key factors: the “cold machin- ing” capability of femtosecond lasers and the high-temperature phase stability of the t’-YSZ material. First, the ultrashort pulse duration of femtosecond lasers fundamentally mitigates thermal diffusion: by confining energy deposition to a nanosecond-scale interaction window, it suppresses HAZ formation and thereby eliminates thermal stress concentration at the TBC/BC interface [ 6 ]. This effect preserves the intrinsic bonding state of the coating system, even for obliquely drilled holes (30◦and 60◦) where stress accumulation is typically more pronounced. Second, the engineered t’-YSZ material maintains excellent phase stability at elevated temperatures. Its highly integrated crystal lattice structure enhances the TBC/BC interfacial bonding strength, circumventing the interfacial weakness caused by tetragonal-to- monoclinic (t→m) phase transformation and associated volume expansion in conventional partially stabilized YSZ [ 29 ]. However, during long- term service under extreme temperatures, continuous thickening of the TGO layer is inevitable. Recent studies have confirmed that progressive TGO growth, coupled with cumulative stress accumulation, is one of the primary failure mechanisms leading to spallation of APS TBCs [ 6 ]. Specifically, TGO growth induces thermal expansion mismatch at the t’-YSZ/TGO and TGO/NiCrCoAlY interfaces. The ther- mal expansion coefficients (TECs) of t’-YSZ, TGO, and NiCr- CoAlY are 10.7 ×10− 6 K− 1 , 8.0 ×10− 6 K− 1 , and 17.5 ×10− 6 K− 1 , respectively [ 31, 32 ]. During cooling from high service tem- peratures to ambient conditions, this TEC mismatch gener- ates substantial “thermal residual stresses” within the brittle TGO layer [ 33 ]. As TGO thickness increases, these accumu- lated internal stresses eventually exceed the interfacial fracture toughness, triggering cracking or delamination at either the t’-YSZ/TGO or TGO/BC interface. According to Evans et al. [ 34 ], the critical TGO thickness for APS-TBC spallation is approximately 6.0 µm; within this range, the thermal cycle life of TBCs decreases exponentially with increasing TGO thickness [ 35 ]. Statistical analysis of TGO thicknesses in Figure 4 yielded the following results: 4.67 µm (nondrilled coating), 4.03 µm (0◦ drilled coating), 4.28 µm (30◦drilled coating), and 3.52 µm (60◦ drilled coating). Notably, the TGO thickness of the 60◦drilled coating was 24.6% lower than that of the nondrilled counterpart. These data indicate that FCHs exert a significant inhibitory effect on TGO growth, with the suppression efficiency enhancing with increasing drilling angle. This phenomenon is attributed to the continuous cooling effect of the gas flowing through the FCHs during thermal cycling: the cooling gas reduces the temperature of the BC surface, slowing the diffusion rate of aluminum (from the BC) and oxygen (from the environment), which are two key processes governing TGO formation and thickening [ 2, 35 ]. For obliquely drilled holes (30◦and 60◦), the larger surface area of the hole walls and more uniform gas film coverage further optimize heat dissipation, leading to more pronounced TGO growth inhibition compared to vertical (0◦) holes. 3.2 Phase Stability of T’-YSZ Coating Under high-temperature conditions, the Y-lean region of t’- YSZ undergoes rapid transformation of the t’ phase into t and cubic (c) phases, with the t phase further transforming into the m phase during cooling [ 14 ]. The content of Y2 O3 exerts a significant impact on the coating’s crystal structure, and accurate microstructural analysis is crucial given these phase transformation behaviors. XRD testing is impractical here due to the exceedingly small area of the film cooling hole edges [ 36 ], whereas Raman spectroscopy enables precise microstructural characterization by virtue of its laser beam’s extremely small diameter (2–4 µm) [ 19, 37, 38 ]. Thus, Raman spectroscopy was selected to investigate microstructural changes at the edges of the film cooling holes. Figure 5 presents the Raman spectra acquired from the coating hole edges after 1617 thermal shock cycles. The results reveal six distinct active vibration modes of the t’ phase, with no characteristic vibrational modes of the m phase detected, as shown in Figure 5 . These six active vibrational modes of the t’ phase at the hole edges correspond to Raman shifts of 145, 260, 319, 468, 606, and 640 cm− 1 , respectively, values that are consistent with the reported characteristic peaks of t’-YSZ [ 39 ]. Compared with the as-sprayed coating, the Raman shifts of the two peaks at 260 and 640 cm− 1 increase after thermal shock cycling, which may be attributed to internal compressive stress generated within the t’-YSZ coating during the cycling process [ 19 ]. Notably, the peak positions remain unchanged with increasing distance from the hole edge and across different drilling angles, though this observation does not inherently indicate constant Y content in the coating. A slight increase in the average bond length, resulting from the substitution of larger Y3 + ions (0.1019 nm) for smaller Zr4 + ions (0.084 nm), would typically reduce bond strength and shift Raman modes toward lower frequencies [ 39 ]. However, oxygen vacancies are generated to maintain lattice electrical neutrality, and the formation of these vacancies reduces the average coordination number of zirconium ions, thereby increas- ing bond strength. This suggests that, regardless of drilling angle, there is no significant change in Y content between the hole edges and the coating interior, and the coating retains a stable t’ phase structure. The complete suppression of the t→m phase transformation in the STHS t’-YSZ coating derives from three intrinsic mechanisms. First, the fully stabilized nontransformable t’ phase with uniform and high Y2 O3 content eliminates Y-lean regions, preventing t’ phase decomposition and subsequent martensitic transformation even at 1200◦C. Second, the unique closed-pore microstructure formed by STHS powder effectively hinders Y3 + diffusion along grain boundaries and significantly slows down phase decompo- sition kinetics. Third, the ultra-high sintering resistance of the STHS coating maintains excellent structural integrity without obvious densification, ensuring long-term composition unifor- mity and phase stability. These synergistic effects eliminate the 3%–5% volume expansion and transformation stress caused by the t→m phase transition in conventional YSZ coatings, ensur- ing reliable structural stability around FCHs during long-term thermal cycling. 3.3 Stability of René N5 Substrate For TBC applications, the composition of René N5 alloy is a critical factor governing TBC durability, as it directly influences the substrate’s mechanical strength, oxidation resistance, and gas corrosion resistance [ 40, 41 ]. To evaluate the alloy’s stability, changes in the content of its major elements were analyzed. Figure 3 presents the EDS analysis results of the René N5 alloy with the 0◦drilled coating after 1617 thermal shock cycles. Notably, a 5 µm-thick TGO layer was formed at the hole edge, primarily composed of Al and O with trace amounts of Ni, Ta, Cr, and Co. This confirms the formation of a protective layer dominated by Al2 O3 at the hole edges, accompanied by a minor oxide layer derived from the oxidation of other alloying elements (including NiO, CrTaO4 , Ni (Al, Co)2 O4 , and (Ni, Co) Cr2 O4 ) [ 42, 43 ]. The TGO layer acts as a barrier to prevent external oxygen from infiltrating the alloy substrate and inducing further oxidation, while also inhibiting the diffusion of substrate cations, thereby mitigating the substrate oxidation process to a considerable degree [ 44, 45 ].
