Introduction The demand for designing and fabricating material surfaces with exceptional hydrophobicity and minimal adhesion has surged across various applications, encompassing energy, environmental concerns, and health considerations. These functional surfaces have effectively addressed performance-related challenges associated with multiple systems, including aircraft wings, wind turbine blades, air conditioning ducts, power transmission cables, and solar panels. Nature has long inspired the development of surface designs with remarkable wettability properties. Examples of these natural wonders include self-cleaning lotus leaves [1], anisotropic superhydrophobic rice leaves [2,3], the captivating rose petal effect [4,5], the wings of the butterflies and cicadas’ flight [6,7], and the intricate fish scale surface [8]. In order to achieve extreme wetting phenomena, it is vital to comprehend the underlying mechanisms driving hydrophobicity, encompassing intricate microstructures and chemical characteristics. Consequently, researchers working in micro and nanofabrication have delved into the realm of bionics engineering to emulate and fabricate superhydrophobic surfaces. Theory of wettability Wettability, a fundamental property that characterizes the interac­ tion between a liquid and a solid surface, is determined by the spreading or adsorption behavior of the liquid on the surface. Central to this phenomenon is the interplay of interaction forces between molecules and molecules [1,2], primarily influenced by the microstructure and chemical composition of the solid surface. The wettability of a solid surface can be quantitatively assessed using the WCA, an important parameter used to gauge the extent of liquid wetting on the surface. As depicted in Fig. 2(a), upon contact between a liquid and a solid surface, the contact line connecting the solid-liquid-gas phases undergoes continuous changes until an equilibrium state is reached. In this equi­ librium state, the static WCA is the angle formed between the tangent along the gas-liquid boundary and the interface of the solid-liquid phase [3,4]. Through the measurement and analysis of the static WCA, valu­ able insights into the wettability characteristics of different surfaces can be obtained. 2.1. Yung’s equation The relationship between WCA and surface finds expression in Young’s equation, an influential formula that describes the wetting characteristics of a smooth or flat surface as illustrated by Eq. (1) [5]. cosθγ = γSV −γSL γLV (1) Within this equation, γSV represents the surface energy of the solid and gas interface, γSL denotes the surface energy between the solid and liquid phases, and γLV signifies the surface energy at the liquid-gas interface. A critical point of reference in assessing hydrophilic and hydropho­ bic properties is a WCA of 90◦. When the WCA is <90◦, the solid surface is deemed hydrophilic, indicating its propensity to be wetted by the liquid. Conversely, when the WCA exceeds 90◦, the solid surface exhibits hydrophobic qualities, impeding effective wetting by the liquid. Extending this concept further, a WCA ranging from 0 to 10◦is referred to as superhydrophilic, while an angle spanning 150◦to 180◦charac­ terizes a superhydrophobic surface. Additional metrics such as the SA/RA and CAH come into play in evaluating superhydrophobic surfaces. The SA or RA refers to the critical angle between the solid surface and the horizontal plane when a droplet rolls on an inclined solid surface. On the other hand, the CAH represents the difference between the advancing and receding angles. The magni­ tude of CAH serves as an indicator of the ease of droplet movement. A smaller value of CAH corresponds to a lower difficulty level in droplet sliding. For instance, the lotus leaf exhibits SA or CAH values below 10◦, facilitating accessible droplet rolling across its surface. In contrast, the rose petal demonstrates distinct behavior with strong adhesion forces effectively anchoring droplets on its surface even under super­ hydrophobic conditions. 2.2. Wenzel model For rough solid surfaces, the characterization of wetting properties often involves utilizing the Wenzel and Cassie-Baxter models, as visually represented in Fig. 2(b) and (c). The Wenzel model considered the roughness of the solid surface by building upon Young’s equation. Ac­ cording to this model, a liquid droplet could thoroughly wet a rough surface, as depicted by Eq. (2) [6]. cosθW = r⋅cosθγ = r⋅γSV −γSL γLV (2) A parameter denoted as “r” encapsulates the concept of the rough­ ness factor, representing the ratio of the actual surface area to the pro­ jected area of a solid rough surface, r ≥1. The symbols θw and θr refer to the WCA on rough and smooth surfaces. Consequently, an increase in roughness may lead to a transition from hydrophilicity to super­ hydrophilicity or hydrophobicity to superhydrophobicity, depending on the initial wetting properties. Mechanism of interaction with materials The interaction between the laser and material varies depending on the materials, which is attributed to the absorption of photon-induced electronic excitations [8]. Particularly under strong light-field condi­ tions, the laser’s electric field distorts the coulomb potential of most neutral atoms. When a fs laser is applied to process metals, many free electrons are generated within the materials. These electrons exhibit a linear absorption of photon energy, leading to instantaneous excitation to high-energy states and subsequent temperature elevation. The high- energy electrons transfer heat to the surrounding atoms within a few picoseconds. As a result, the temperature instantaneously surpasses the melting point, causing the metal to undergo liquefied vaporization. The high-pressure environment gives rise to the formation of plasma, resulting in the removal of metal and the creation of micro-nano struc­ tures [9,10]. In contrast, non-metallic materials, such as semiconductors, media­ tors, and insulators, possess limited free electrons. However, these ma­ terials can generate free electrons through ionization (photoionization and shock ionization) [11]. When many free electrons are aggregated, they exhibit transient metal-like properties. Their interaction with the lattice triggers both thermal phase transitions (melting and gasification) and non-thermal phase transitions (electrostatic ablation and coulomb explosion) [11]. The thermal phase transition mechanism involves the heating and subsequent melting of the lattice. In contrast, the non- thermal phase transition is characterized by plasma expansion, with the Coulomb explosion playing a dominant role [11]. For a compre­ hensive depiction of the multi-scale model illustrating the interaction between fs lasers and materials, refer to Fig. 3. For femtosecond and picosecond laser processing, the interaction among the electrons is much faster than the electron-phonon coupling relaxation time [12]. Thus, Thus, the energy that the electrons absorb cannot immediately transfer to the atoms in the lattice. The electron- electron interaction can only raise the local electron temperature to produce nonequilibrium ablation. In contrast, the atoms are confined to the lattice position with almost no heat transfer, resulting in a small heat-affected zone and smooth edges of the ablation zone [8,12]. 3.2. Equipment According to the classification based on the duration of laser pulses, laser processing can be categorized into different time scales: femto­ second, picosecond, nanosecond, and millisecond. When the pulse duration is equal to or shorter than the picosecond level, it falls under the category of ultrafast lasers [12]. Among these ultrashort pulse lasers, the fs laser stands out with a pulse duration of 10−15 fs [13]. Fig. 4(a) shows a typical fs laser system consisting of four key com­ ponents: a femtosecond laser generator, an optical system, a control system, and a 3D mobile station. The attenuator is employed to regulate the laser’s energy, while the electromechanical shutter controls the opening and closing of the laser beam. The wave plate (λ/4 and λ/2) adjusts the polarization state. The beam direction can be altered using a mirror, and the laser beam can be focused with the assistance of a lens. A CCD camera facilitates real-time optical microscopic imaging. The en­ ergy distribution of fs laser beams primarily follows a Gaussian profile, which can be adjusted to various spatial distributions such as a square beam with a flat top, circular beam with a flat top, vortex beam, or Bessel beam, as depicted in Fig. 4(b) [11]. [PAGE 5] Journal of Manufacturing Processes 109 (2024) 250–287 254 3.3. Advantages Fs laser processing offers several notable advantages in the field of material processing. Firstly, it exhibits a small heat-affected area, ensuring high precision and excellent processing quality. This is attrib­ uted to the extremely short pulse duration, significantly smaller than the lattice’s heat exchange time. As a result, fs laser processing is often called “cold processing” due to its minimal thermal effects. Secondly, the wide range of applications is another key strength of fs laser processing. With pulse durations on the order of femtoseconds, these lasers can generate peak powers as high as 1014 W. This immense power enables the processing of multiple materials, including metals, ceramics, glass, polymers, and semiconductors. The versatility of fs lasers makes them suitable for various materials across different industries [15]. Further­ more, fs laser processing showcases the ability to fabricate non-flat and complex patterns. Microstructures with distinct morphological charac­ teristics can be achieved on irregular surfaces by adjusting specific pa­ rameters of the fs laser, such as laser fluence, scan speed, frequency, number of scans, and scanning spacing. This capability opens up new avenues for creating intricate and tailored structures, expanding the possibilities of design and manufacturing processes. 4. Microstructures 4.1. Laser-induced periodic surface structures 4.1.1. Generation and evolution LIPSS is a fascinating phenomenon characterized by the emergence of nano-ripple structures comprising closely spaced and periodically arranged stripes on the material surface. The discovery of LIPSS can be traced back to the pioneering work of Birnbaum in the 1960s, who observed these novel surface structures when employing a pulsed ruby laser to ablate germanium at an incidence angle of 75◦[16]. The intri­ cate formation mechanism of LIPSS is predominantly attributed to two key factors: the non-uniform distribution of laser energy across the material surface and the interference of scattered waves induced by the incident laser beam. This generation mechanism has gained widespread acceptance among researchers as the most plausible explanation [17]. Notably, the LIPSS’s direction is strongly influenced by the LIPSS type and the processed material. At the same time, the spatial period of LIPSS tends to be shorter than the wavelength of the incident laser [18]. In general, the LSFL tends to align perpendicular to the polarization di­ rection of the incident laser beam, whereas the HSFL aligns parallel to the polarization direction [19]. However, for certain materials like transparent media and superalloys, the LSFL aligns parallel to the po­ larization direction of the laser, while the HSFL aligns perpendicular to it [20,21]. LIPSS can be classified into two categories based on their spatial frequencies: low spatial frequency LIPSS (LSFL) and high spatial fre­ quency LIPSS (HSFL). LSFL refers to LIPSS with periods ranging from less than the incident laser wavelength to greater than half of the inci­ dent laser wavelength. In contrast, HSFL denotes LIPSS with periods shorter than half of the incident laser wavelength [22]. The formation mechanism of HSFL remains controversial, while the scattered-light model and the surface plasmon polariton model are used to explain the formation of LSFL. The scattered light model suggests that LSFL is formed by the interference between the incident laser and the scattered light from the ablated surface [23]. The relationship between the period of LSFL and the incident laser wavelength can be mathematically expressed through Eq. (5) [24], as depicted in Fig. 5(a), providing valuable insights into the underlying physics governing the formation of LSFL structures on the material surface. Λ = λ 1 ± sinθ (5) where Λ is the period of LSFL, λ is the wavelength of the incident laser, θ is the tilt angle of the incident laser beam, and ± represents the pre- scattered and back-scattered light, respectively. The generation of the LSFL period depends on the surface roughness. The incidence angle and polarization direction affect the period and direction of LSFL, respec­ tively. An alternative model known as the surface plasmon polariton model offers an intriguing explanation for the formation of LSFL. The surface plasmon polariton model considers that many free electrons caused by fs laser irradiation form a plasma layer. Fs laser further causes the col­ lective oscillation of plasma to form plasmons, as well as the periodic distribution of light field and energy deposition of free electrons [23]. To quantify the period of LSFL in accordance with the surface plasmon polariton model, Eq. (6) provides a mathematical formula that accounts for the wavelength of the surface plasmon polariton (λspp) [25]. Fig. 5(b) visually portrays the underlying mechanisms involved in the formation of LSFL as described by the surface plasmon polariton model. Λ = λ λ λspp ± sinθ (6) where λspp is the wavelength of surface plasmon polariton. Beyond the surface plasmon polariton model, various other theories have been proposed to elucidate the formation mechanism of laser-induced peri­ odic surface structures. These include the self-organization [26], which emphasizes the spontaneous ordering of material under the influence of laser irradiation. The Coulomb explosion [27] explores the role of electrostatic repulsion in generating LSFL, and the second harmonic [28] investigates nonlinear optical processes as a potential cause of HSFL formation. Wettability The wettability characteristics of LIPSS are notably influenced by the accumulated laser fluence, which, in turn, is intricately linked to several key factors, such as laser pulses, laser fluence, scanning speed, and scanning spacing. Zemaitis et al. [34] aimed to explore the intricate relationship between LIPSS generated through cumulative laser fluence and the hydrophobic properties exhibited by stainless steel surfaces. The gradual shift from the superhydrophilic (WCA = 4◦) to the super­ hydrophobic (WCA ≈150◦) was observed with the increase in laser fluence, decrease in the hatch distance, and scanning speed (after 15 days of laser irrad). These factors effectively increased the accumulated laser fluence. As illustrated in Fig. 6(a), it was observed that manipu­ lating the laser fluence within the range of 0.08–0.64 J/cm2, along with a scanning speed of 1000 mm/s and a hatch distance of 5 μm, signifi­ cantly elevated the accumulated laser fluence to the extent that resulted in a notable enhancement in the WCA, reaching approximately 135◦. Furthermore, by further refining the experimental parameters, specif­ ically by reducing the scanning speed to 50 mm/s and decreasing the hatch distance from 110 μm to 10 μm, the accumulated laser fluence was increased from 240 J/cm2 to 2640 J/cm2, ultimately culminating in the attainment of a remarkable WCA of 150◦. These findings underscore the intricate interplay between various parameters, shedding light on the significant role of cumulative laser fluence in shaping the hydrophobic properties of LIPSS on stainless steel surfaces. Rajendran et al. [35] successfully transformed the glass surface from a hydrophilic state with a WCA of 42◦to a superhydrophilic state, achieving a WCA of <5◦. This transformation was accomplished by depositing HSFL with a period of approximately 380 nm across a significant area of the glass surface. In situations where superhydrophobic properties are desired for specific glass areas, utilizing this processing parameter in conjunction with low surface energy substances to achieve superhydrophobic modification. [36], the researchers aimed to explore the impact of repetition frequency, in conjunction with accumulated laser fluence, on the morphological evolution exhibited by 316L stain­ less steel. Through their experimental investigations, it was observed that an increase in the repetition frequency within the range of 100–1000 kHz, coupled with varying levels of accumulated laser fluence spanning from 178 J/cm2 to 1143 J/cm2, led to a noticeable enlarge­ ment in the size of the micro-pillar structures formed on the material’s surface. This phenomenon is visually depicted in Fig. 6(b). Notably, at an accumulated laser fluence of 178 J/cm2, LIPSS possessing a nanoparticle-covered surface were successfully fabricated. With the accu­ mulated laser fluence increased to 423 J/cm2, 831 J/cm2, and 1143 J/ cm2, the LIPSS transformed into a micro-pillar structure with a larger size, and nanoparticles still covered the surface. After 30 days, the sur­ face with a dense micropillar structure (423 J/cm2) presented an impressive high WCA exceeding 160◦and accompanied by an SA of 11◦, exhibiting a strong self-cleaning effect.
