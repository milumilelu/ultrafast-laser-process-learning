[PAGE 1] Quantitative prediction and degradation mechanism of CFRP–TC4 adhesive joints under hygrothermal aging Haichao Liu, Ruihua Liu, Chang He, Miao Yu, Yaolin Wang & Ziyang Ding Adhesive joints between carbon fiber reinforced polymer (CFRP) and titanium alloy (TC4) are widely used in aerospace and rail transit structures.They offer excellent specific strength and lightweight advantages.However, their long-term performance is often threatened by hygrothermal coupling environments.This study systematically investigates the mechanical degradation of CFRP–TC4 single- lap adhesive joints under varying temperature (40, 60, and 80 °C), humidity (95% RH and immersion), and aging time (0–720 h).The novelty of this work lies in quantitatively identifying humidity as the dominant factor affecting joint strength through response surface modeling, supported by experimental validation.Results show that both failure strength and stiffness decrease with aging, with a strength loss of over 40% after 720 h at 80 °C/100% RH.Fracture morphology evolves from interfacial or matrix failure to cohesive failure, while SEM and FTIR analyses reveal that moisture ingress and epoxy hydrolysis induce adhesive plasticization and functional group transformation.These findings provide a scientific basis for durability design and service life prediction of CFRP–metal bonded structures in humid and high-temperature applications. Keywords CFRP–TC4 joints, Hygrothermal coupling, Mechanical degradation, Fracture mechanism, Response surface methodology Literature review The demand for lightweight structures with high strength is driving the widespread use of carbon fiber reinforced polymers (CFRP) and titanium alloys (Ti–6Al–4 V, TC4) in aerospace, rail transit, and new energy vehicles1–4. CFRPs exhibit excellent specific strength, corrosion resistance, and fatigue performance5,6, while TC4 combines high strength-to-weight ratio with good thermal stability7,8. For structural connections, adhesive bonding has emerged as an important technique for joining CFRP–metal hybrid structures owing to its lightweight nature, uniform stress distribution, and process flexibility9–11. However, in service environments such as aerospace and rail transit, these structures are often subjected to alternating temperature and humidity conditions, making the durability of adhesive joints a key factor limiting their service life12–14. Previous studies have shown that hygrothermal environments can lead to adhesive plasticization, interfacial debonding, and matrix degradation, ultimately reducing the mechanical performance of adhesive joints15,16. Research has evolved from focusing on single environmental factors to exploring coupled temperature– humidity–medium conditions, allowing systematic evaluation of joint strength, fatigue, and fracture behavior under hygrothermal and salt-spray exposures17.Moreover, extreme temperatures have been found to alter the load-bearing mechanisms and failure modes of CFRP/titanium tubular joints18.However, the combined effects of temperature, humidity, and aging duration on the durability of CFRP–TC4 adhesive joints remain insufficiently understood, motivating the present study. At the mechanistic level, multi-scale characterization has deepened understanding of hygrothermal aging effects on epoxy adhesives.Zhang et al. \24 and Han et al.25 found that moisture preferentially penetrates the interfacial region, inducing matrix plasticization and hydrolysis, which lower glass transition temperature and fracture toughness.Galvez et al. \26 and Banea et al.27 confirmed that hygrothermal exposure modifies adhesive functional groups, leading to macroscopic strength loss.Further analyses using FTIR, DMA, and microscopy revealed the evolution of chemical and structural damage under severe aging17.Surface treatment studies have shown that appropriate modification can significantly enhance joint strength and resistance to hygrothermal degradation28–30.However, most existing studies focus on CFRP–aluminum or CFRP–steel joints, leaving the long-term hygrothermal durability of CFRP–TC4 systems insufficiently explored.This study hypothesizes that humidity plays a dominant role in the mechanical degradation of CFRP–TC4 adhesive joints under coupled hygrothermal conditions.The objective is to experimentally quantify this effect and establish a predictive model to support durability design and service-life estimation of CFRP–titanium hybrid structures. From a methodological perspective, statistical modeling and machine learning have recently been introduced into durability prediction of adhesive joints. Karimi et al.32,33 developed hybrid approaches combining experimental data with machine learning, enabling rapid prediction of CFRP/Al joint performance under hygrothermal aging. Meng et al.34 and Liu et al.35 further applied multi-scale modeling and life prediction methods, identifying the interactive influences of temperature, humidity, and exposure time. Nevertheless, quantitative studies focusing on CFRP–TC4 adhesive systems across multiple environmental variables remain scarce. Integrating experimental data with statistical modeling offers a promising path to elucidate degradation mechanisms under hygrothermal exposure and to support service life evaluation and reliability-based design in engineering applications. Although the hygrothermal durability of CFRP–metal joints has been extensively investigated, the bulk of existing work centres on CFRP–aluminium or CFRP–steel systems; quantitative studies on CFRP–TC4 titanium bonded joints remain scarce. More importantly, current literature predominantly correlates temperature, humidity and exposure time with macroscopic strength loss, while the chemical evolution of the adhesive (e.g., ester hydrolysis, etherification) and its causal link to mechanical degradation are seldom addressed. Consequently, there is no mechanism-based, cross-scale modelling framework that couples molecular-level changes to macroscopic load-bearing capacity. This study therefore advances the following hypothesis: Strength degradation of CFRP–TC4 single-lap joints under hygrothermal conditions is primarily driven by defined chemical reactions within the epoxy adhesive. These reactions can be quantitatively tracked by FTIR spectroscopy and subsequently embedded in a Response Surface Model to predict residual strength with mechanistic insight.To test this hypothesis, we designed a multi-factor hygrothermal ageing matrix (temperature × humidity × time) and integrated mechanical testing, fractography, FTIR-based chemical quantification and Response Surface Methodology into a single analysis workflow. The principal novelties are: First systematic tracking of adhesive chemical evolution (ester consumption, carbonyl/ether growth) in CFRP–TC4 joints by FTIR, explicitly linking peak-intensity changes to strength loss.Chemistry-informed RSM: unlike purely empirical fits, the regression model incorporates FTIR metrics as proxy variables, improving predictive accuracy (error ≤ 6.53%) and mechanistic interpretability.Quantitative ranking of degradation drivers: humidity > temperature > exposure time, providing a clear hierarchy for durability design.Transferable experimental–analytical framework that can be extended to other CFRP–metal systems exposed to coupled environmental loads.By bridging chemical kinetics and macro-scale mechanics, this work offers a new pathway for mechanism-based life prediction and material-oriented durability design of CFRP–titanium bonded structures in hygrothermal service environments. Main contributions Based on the identified research gaps, this study focuses on CFRP–TC4 single-lap adhesive joints and designs hygrothermal aging experiments under multiple temperature (40℃, 60℃, and 80℃), humidity (95% and immersion), and aging time (0–720 h) conditions. An experimental database was established using this multi- temperature/multi-humidity/multi-duration matrix, and quasi-static tensile tests were conducted to reveal the mechanical degradation behavior. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) were employed to characterize failure morphologies and adhesive functional group variations, thereby elucidating the influence mechanisms of hygrothermal environments at both microscopic and molecular levels. Furthermore, a response surface methodology (RSM)-based strength prediction model was developed to quantitatively analyze the significance and interactions of temperature, humidity, and aging time on joint durability. The findings of this work provide both experimental evidence and theoretical support for the durability design and service life prediction of CFRP–metal adhesive structures under hygrothermal conditions. Hygrothermal aging mMatrix For thermal aging, a blast drying oven (Yinggong DHG-9040) was used. The control panel was set to the designed aging temperature, and once the chamber temperature reached the target condition, the cured specimens were placed inside. After the designated aging duration, the specimens were removed and cooled to room temperature. For hygrothermal coupled aging, a temperature–humidity alternating test chamber (WS-1000, Weisi Equipment Co., Ltd.) was employed. The desired temperature was set on the control panel, and deionized water was used as the humidity source. The chamber’s temperature control system ensured precise heating and stable thermal conditions throughout the test process. In terms of humidity control, a relative humidity of 95%RH was achieved and maintained using the chamber’s built-in humidity regulation system. To simulate immersion (water bath) conditions, containers filled with deionized water were placed inside the chamber. After both temperature and humidity reached the specified test conditions, the cured specimens were placed in the chamber for aging and removed upon completion of the exposure period. Fixture and specimen fabrication A custom fixture was designed for the fabrication of single-lap joint specimens, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The fixture consisted of three main components: the fixture body, tightening bolts with clamping blocks, and spacers. The clamping blocks and bolts secured the specimens in place, while GFRP spacers of specified thickness were used to control the adhesive layer thickness. To ensure uniform pressure distribution during curing, rectangular acrylic plates were used on both sides of the specimens. A layer of Teflon tape was applied to the fixture’s inner surfaces to prevent bonding between the fixture and excess adhesive, as well as to facilitate demolding after curing. The fixture was assembled with bolts, allowing excess adhesive to be squeezed out and ensuring uniform joint thickness. All specimens were bonded under standardized procedures in a clean environment at room temperature (25 ± 3℃, 50 ± 5%RH). The adhesive layer thickness in single-lap joint structures can be readily controlled, and the stress distribution is relatively uniform. Compared to other joint configurations, this design demonstrates broader applicability. Fig. 1. High and low temperature humid heat alternating test chamber. [PAGE 6] Plasma treatment of substrate surfaces Surface Treatment Procedure. 1. Pre-cleaning. Both TC4 and CFRP substrates were first cleaned with acetone to remove surface contaminants such as grease, dust, and residues. 2. Sandblasting (for TC4 substrate). The TC4 surface was sandblasted using alumina particles (Al₂O₃) to increase surface roughness and promote mechanical interlocking. After sandblasting, the specimens were thoroughly cleaned again with acetone to eliminate residual abrasive particles. 3. Plasma Treatment (for TC4 and CFRP substrates). The bonding areas of both substrates were treated with an atmospheric-pressure plasma surface-treatment unit (Model TFR02-PL-3500). Ambient air was ionized by a high-voltage power supply, and the plasma was ejected through a rotating nozzle. The nozzle-to-surface distance was maintained at 20 mm, with the power set to 800 Wand the treatment duration of 90 s.As shown in Figure 5. Plasma treatment aimed to: Create a micro-pitted, rough topography on the CFRP surface.Enhance surface wettability by increasing surface free energy and generating free radicals.Introduce oxygen- and nitrogen- containing functional groups to provide additional active bonding sites. Substrate surface sandblasting treatment Sandblasting employs compressed air as the power source to project a high-velocity stream of abrasive particles (e.g., quartz sand, alumina, or iron grit) onto the workpiece surface. Under impact and cutting action of these rapidly moving abrasives, the surface attains a controlled level of cleanliness and roughness. This treatment improves the mechanical characteristics of the surface, enhances adhesion between the substrate and the adhesive, and increases the durability of the bonded joint. A sandblasting unit was employed to treat the bond area of the TC4 substrates (CFRP was excluded because the process would damage its surface). The parameters were: Abrasive: #80 alumina grit.Air pressure: 0.8 MPa. Nozzle angle: 90°.Blasting time: 10 s. Before blasting, the TC4 bond area was wiped with acetone; after blasting the specimens were immersed in fresh acetone and ultrasonically cleaned for 3 min.
