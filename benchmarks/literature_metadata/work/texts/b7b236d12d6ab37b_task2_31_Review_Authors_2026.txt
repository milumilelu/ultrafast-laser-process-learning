The core of film cooling lies in optimizing the interaction between the coolant and the main flow, including reducing coolant penetration and enhancing wall- adherence effects, which mainly depends on film holes. The geometric structure of the film holes directly de­ termines the initial formation and subsequent development of the jet outflow, playing a decisive role in cooling effectiveness as well as aerodynamic loss. Traditional cylindrical holes suffer from issues like flow separation at high blowing ratios and limited coverage, making them inadequate for the extreme thermal demands of next- generation engines. According to incomplete statistics, research to date has produced roughly 125 docu­ mented hole variations, broadly classified into three categories: the intrinsic shape of the film hole, auxiliary structures around the film hole, and combined film hole configurations. Recently, artificial intelligence tech­ nologies have provided novel insights for film hole optimization, establishing systematic optimization workflows through surrogate models and advanced algorithms. However, existing studies still lack in-depth analysis of the underlying cooling mechanisms of film holes and systematic summarization of optimization methodologies. Accordingly, this review examines decades of progress in film hole structures based on the aforementioned taxonomy, summarizes their respective cooling mechanisms, and synthesizes prevailing optimization ap­ proaches. The aim is to provide a systematic theoretical reference and technical prospects for high-performance film cooling design. 1. Introduction Since the beginning of the 21st century, the global energy transition and the need to address climate change have reached an international consensus. Organizations such as the International Civil Aviation Or­ ganization (ICAO) have consequently established long-term carbon reduction goals for the aviation sector, imposing stringent requirements on the efficiency and environmental performance of propulsion systems. Improving thermal efficiency is a central strategy to reduce fuel con­ sumption and carbon emissions, which directly relies on the continuous increase of Turbine Inlet Temperature (TIT) [1]. Studies indicate that for every 56 K increase in turbine inlet temperature, engine output power can rise by approximately 10% and thermal efficiency by 0.5%–1%. Moreover, realizing about 70% of the potential for further temperature increases depends on advances in cooling technology [2]. As shown in Fig. 1, the evolution of advanced engines across generations has been accompanied by an average increase of 200–300 K in the TIT. Since the advent of third-generation engines, the TIT has consistently exceeded the allowable limits of contemporary blade alloy materials. Currently, advanced military engines such as the F135 have reached turbine inlet temperatures around 2200 K, far exceeding the safe operating limit of the blade substrate material [3,4]. Against this backdrop, aside from applying thermal barrier coating (TBC) for partial insulation, blade cooling technology has become the essential means of thermal protec­ tion to ensure the reliable operation of turbine blades in harsh high- temperature environments. To ensure structural integrity and service life under extreme heat fluxes, modern turbine blades employ a combination of cooling strategies. Internal cooling typically utilizes serpentine passages to enhance convective heat transfer, often combined with impingement cooling, in which high- velocity coolant jets impinge vertically onto the inner wall, achieving extremely high heat transfer coefficients in localized regions. Effusion cooling (also known as porous laminate cooling) and impingement/ effusion compound cooling structures utilize dense arrays of micro-holes to achieve uniform coolant transpiration, serving the dual functions of enhancing internal heat transfer and forming a thermal barrier on the external surface. Transpiration cooling, a highly promising active ther­ mal protection concept, achieves uniform coolant permeation across the entire surface through porous media fabricated via additive manufacturing (AM), theoretically attaining near-ideal cooling effi­ ciency. These technical approaches are comprehensively reviewed in the articles by Yeranee et al. [5] and Wang et al. [6]. Beyond the cooling techniques discussed above, film cooling remains the most mature and broadly applicable blade external surface cooling technology in current engineering practice. Its core principle involves ejecting cooling air from discretely distributed holes or slots on the blade surface and injecting it as jets into the mainstream gas, thereby forming a continuously renewed, well-attached low-temperature film on the blade's external surface that effectively isolates the high-temperature gas from direct contact with the wall. The physical essence of this technology lies in optimizing the mixing mechanism between the coolant jets and the mainstream gas. The key objectives are to suppress excessive jet liftoff and penetration into the mainstream, enhance film attachment to the wall and spanwise coverage, and maximize the protective distance of the cooling film. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the geometry of the film holes decisively governs the initial formation and subsequent down­ stream development of the coolant jet, playing a decisive role in both the cooling effectiveness and the associated aerodynamic loss. Since Gold­ stein et al. [7] conducted systematic research on classic cylindrical holes in 1969, this hole configuration has been extensively studied over the past decades, achieving high technological maturity and successful application in third and fourth-generation aero-engines. However, as turbine inlet temperatures approach 2200–2300 K in next-generation engines, the cooling film generated by traditional cylindrical holes be­ comes susceptible to detachment and entrainment due to the transverse pressure gradient and flow field shear of the mainstream. This results in poor cooling effectiveness and limited coverage, rendering such holes inadequate for future thermal protection requirements. The effectiveness of film cooling is influenced by numerous geo­ metric parameters, including the injection angle, compound angle, and exit-area ratio. Among these, the macroscopic film hole shape is the predominant factor controlling the near-wall flow field structure and the resultant cooling performance. In recent decades, researchers have aimed to enhance jet adherence, spanwise coverage, and mixing char­ acteristics by innovatively designing the film hole exit geometry, adding auxiliary structures, internal flow modifications, and multi-hole con­ figurations, thereby achieving superior cooling performance under identical coolant mass flow rates. Recently, Li et al. [8] reviewed the research progress and application of kidney vortices in film cooling, revealing their influence mechanism on cooling characteristics. Wang et al. [9] surveyed the development of turbine loss models, with a focus on the impact of film cooling. Liu et al. [10] analyzed the performance differences of film cooling under the influence of secondary flows in different blade profiles. By shaping distinct vortex structures and flow patterns, different hole configurations exhibit unique film-formation mechanisms and cooling advantages, sustaining their status as a persistent research hotspot. [PAGE 3] It is well recognized that while complex shaped holes demonstrate significantly better cooling performance than cylindrical holes in labo­ ratory studies, their intricate geometries pose severe manufacturing challenges. This substantially limits their large-scale application on in- service turbine blades. Undoubtedly, advancements in AM technology will significantly broaden the engineering application of complex sha­ ped holes and hold promise for the successful implementation of effu­ sion cooling and transpiration cooling on turbine blades. This progression represents a natural scientific evolution of turbine blade cooling methods, from film cooling to effusion cooling and ultimately to transpiration cooling. Correspondingly, changes in film hole diameter and density, along with advancements in porous materials, will serve as direct manifestations of this paradigm shift [5]. It can be reasonably anticipated that research in this area will proliferate over the next 10 to 20 years, addressing numerous current gaps in the field. It must be acknowledged, however, that while AM demonstrates significant potential in the fabrication of complex shaped holes, related research remains in its nascent stages. A major contribution of AM lies in its ability to shape the internal and inlet structures of film holes. Due to accessibility constraints, traditional manufacturing methods are largely limited to shaping the visible portions of film holes. AM, however, al­ lows for tailoring the hole inlet geometry to improve coolant distribu­ tion [11]. Nevertheless, challenges associated with AM, such as manufacturing dimensional inaccuracies, surface roughness, structural strength limitations, and pore clogging, significantly constrain the practical application of these ideal cooling methods. Consequently, a critical challenge for heat transfer researchers is to maximize film cooling performance through structural optimization while respecting the constraints of existing manufacturing capabilities. In recent years, the advancement of artificial intelligence has facilitated the application of sophisticated optimization methods to film hole design. A relatively well-established optimization workflow has emerged, encompassing steps from surrogate model construction to the use of advanced algo­ rithms for identifying optimal solutions. In this context, Zhang et al. [12] reviewed optimization studies for both film-cooling and internal cooling structures in gas turbines. Liu et al. [13] summarized the progress in applying machine learning methods to turbomachinery. However, a systematic consolidation of the diverse optimization methods for film holes is still lacking. Furthermore, studies on film hole structures, auxiliary structures, and the interference effects between holes also require timely summarization. In summary, driven by the dual demands for high performance and durability in aero-engines, the innovation and optimization of film hole structures have emerged as one of the most active and promising research frontiers in the field of film cooling. Accordingly, this paper aims to systematically review the research progress in film hole struc­ ture design and optimization methodologies over recent decades. Drawing from approximately 400 papers published over the past 30 years on various film hole types, this review selects about 160 pertinent studies, which encompass roughly 40 widely investigated film hole configurations. Preliminary statistics indicate that research can be categorized into approximately 45–70 distinct hole structures, 35–55 auxiliary structures, and 20–35 combined hole configurations. Various major film hole structures are presented in Fig. 2, while the proportional representation of these three categories is shown in Fig. 3, visually illustrating the narrative framework of this study. Since different structures enhance film cooling performance through distinct mecha­ nisms, this review focuses on analyzing these underlying mechanisms of various film hole types and systematically compiling the corresponding optimization methods. The goal is to provide a reference for researchers engaged in film cooling design and optimization, concluding with an outlook on future directions for film hole structure optimization. 2. Fan-shaped holes Following Goldstein et al.'s pioneering demonstration of the superior cooling performance of fan-shaped holes over cylindrical holes in the 1970s [16], this diffusing geometry has remained a research focus for decades. Its flow characteristics, which reduce jet penetration and enhance lateral coverage, are key to this sustained interest. Conse­ quently, extensive research has yielded a profound understanding of their cooling performance. Research exploring the performance boundaries of fan-shaped holes has elucidated their underlying enhancement mechanisms and key influencing factors. Gritsch et al. [17] first systematically verified the significant advantage of expanded exits (e.g., fan-shaped and laidback fan-shaped holes) in enhancing downstream thermal protection under harsh, high mainstream Mach number conditions. They correctly iden­ tified the mainstream Mach number and flow direction as key performance-determining variables, thereby establishing important boundary conditions for subsequent studies. Detailed measurements [18] indicate that pressure loss is largely insensitive to changes in fan- shaped hole geometry, with discharge coefficients (CD) typically ranging from 0.8 to 0.9. Adverse effects from compound angles at high M can be partially mitigated by adopting asymmetric hole arrangements. Qenawy et al. [19] investigated the influence of crossflow inlet condi­ tions on the vortex structure within fan-shaped holes. Under crossflow inlet conditions (Fig. 4(b)), the coolant undergoes a sudden helical motion at the hole entrance, forming a vortex tube inside the hole. This vortex tube develops along the hole and interacts with the mainstream at the exit. An upstream horseshoe vortex forms at the leading edge of the hole, while an asymmetric, downstream Counter Rotating Vortex Pair (CRVP) develops at the spanwise edges. They noted that the windward side promotes lateral spread through high-momentum coolant, whereas the leeward side suffers from momentum deficit due to the in-hole vortex tube, thereby altering the exit flow profile. While numerous studies, including this one, have examined the impact of crossflow on coolant distribution and cooling effectiveness, the vast majority of research focusing on film hole geometry employs perpendicular inlet conditions to isolate the influence of crossflow from the key research variables. Research on geometric parameter optimization has indicated a transition in the primary design criterion from the expansion angle to the exit width. Liu et al. [20] investigated the effects of the hole exit lateral expansion angle and spanwise width on the film cooling effec­ tiveness (η) of diffuser-shaped holes. Fig. 5 presents schematic structures of LBFS and FS holes. Compared to FS holes, LBFS holes generally pro­ vide wider and more uniform film coverage, leading to superior cooling performance. However, at a lateral expansion angle of 15◦, the cooling performance of LBFS holes is reduced to the same level as that of FS holes across all momentum ratios. At this angle, the η near the hole exit is even lower than that of FS holes. Increasing the spanwise width of the hole exit enhances η for both types of diffuser holes, attributable to the broader film coverage at the exit. Nevertheless, an excessive spanwise width at the hole exit (e.g., 3.4D) can lead to mainstream ingestion, thereby reducing film cooling effectiveness. Wei et al. [21] investigated a full-coverage arrangement of fan-shaped holes with a fixed exit width on a flat plate. They determined that fan-shaped holes with a 25◦ inclination angle and a 13◦lateral expansion angle yield the most uni­ form film cooling effectiveness. The row-by-row interaction of coolant jets benefits film cooling performance, as the kidney vortex pairs (KVP) can be partially counteracted by secondary vortices originating from holes in the subsequent row. They also provided correlations for the segmental area-averaged adiabatic film cooling effectiveness and the hole CD. However, the superiority of fan-shaped holes is not absolute and is highly conditional on specific operating conditions and geometric pa­ rameters. For instance, Zhu et al. [22] demonstrated that dustpan- shaped holes (a variant of fan-shaped holes) increase the optimal blowing ratio. In contrast, Liu et al. [23] noted that at a high M on the blade suction side (SS), the cooling effectiveness of such holes decreases due to flow separation. This highlights the limitations of simply diffusing exits on complex curved surfaces. More critically, experiments by Li et al. [24] under rotating conditions challenged the conventional wis­ dom. They revealed a reversal in performance: at a high M with a rotational speed of 600 rpm, cylindrical holes outperformed fan-shaped holes in cooling effectiveness. Conclusions derived from stationary flat plate studies may not be directly applicable to rotating blades. Furthermore, the excellent performance of fan-shaped holes comes at the cost of two inherent risks: 1. Hot gas ingestion. At low blowing ratios or during engine transients (e.g., low-power operation), the larger exit area and lower jet momentum of fan-shaped holes may be insufficient to resist the mainstream pressure. This can lead to the entrainment or even ingestion of hot gas into the cooling holes. 2. Localized overheating and compromised structural integrity at the hole exit. The diffused exit removes more material, leading to a thinner wall and a larger heated surface area. Concurrently, the convective cooling along the hole's inner edge may be weakened due to flow diffusion. These factors collectively make the hole exit region a local hot spot, subject to elevated thermal stress and ablation risks. This poses significant challenges to the material's temperature resistance and the durability of TBC. Given the numerous and often complex findings from past research, establishing a standardized film hole as a new benchmark for perfor­ mance comparison has become an urgent requirement to advance the field. As Schroeder and Thole [25] noted, the utility of continuing to use cylindrical holes as the benchmark for evaluating advanced hole con­ figurations is diminishing. They prospectively defined and experimen­ tally characterized the “7–7-7 hole”—a standard laidback fan-shaped hole with specific geometric parameters: 7◦streamwise and lateral expansion angles, an area ratio of 2.5, and a hole length and spacing of 6D (see Fig. 6). They provided detailed experimental data on the streamwise and laterally averaged η of this baseline hole across a range of blowing ratios, momentum flux ratios (IR), and mainstream turbu­ lence intensities. These studies collectively affirm the value of this established baseline hole. Moreover, innovation based on the fan-shaped hole archetype has never ceased. The combined-hole-slot (CHS) proposed by Hu et al. [29], which connects two fan-shaped hole exits (Fig. 7), demonstrated supe­ rior cooling performance only at low M on the PS. On the SS, however, both combined configurations significantly enhanced η across a range of M, with the DB-VS structure showing the best performance. This demonstrates that innovation based on mature fan-shaped hole struc­ tures remains a viable path for performance enhancement. In another example, Chen et al. [30] modified the inlet of a 7–7-7 hole into a curved passage (Fig. 8), creating a so-called curved shaped hole. This design leverages the interaction between the kidney vortex and a Dean vortex, which rotates in the opposite sense. Their results showed that at M = 1.5, the curved hole achieved an area-averaged η approximately 35% higher than the baseline 7–7-7 hole. In summary, research on fan-shaped holes has primarily focused on the influence of key geometric parameters—injection angle, exit width, and lateral and streamwise expansion angles—on film cooling charac­ teristics. This extensive and relatively mature body of work has estab­ lished fan-shaped holes as the most engineering-relevant structure after cylindrical holes.
