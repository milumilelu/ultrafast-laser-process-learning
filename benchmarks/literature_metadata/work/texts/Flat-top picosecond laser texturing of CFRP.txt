However, dissimilar bonding is highly sensitive to surface conditions: CFRP surfaces exhibit low surface energy and may retain mold-release agents, and conventional pretreatments have limitations in control- ling and homogenizing surface topography and chemical activation, which undermines interfacial adhesion and reliability. This work pre- sents a 355 nm flat-top picosecond-laser approach that integrates bioinspired hierarchical texturing with surface activation, enabling programmable construction of tree-frog–inspired “plateau-connected-groove” patterns and concomitantly enhancing surface polarity. Roughness and feature depth increase with fluence; the water contact angle decreases to 8.0°; and x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy indicates higher fractions of oxidized carbon species, while the Raman I_D/I_G ratio remains essentially unchanged. INTRODUCTION Carbon-fiber-reinforced polymer (CFRP) composites have become indispensable in weight-critical structures owing to their high specific strength and stiffness, fatigue and corrosion resistance, and multifunctional designability.1–3 Driven by declining fiber costs and stringent efficiency and emission targets, their adoption has expanded from aerospace to automotive, wind energy, civil infra- structure, and sports engineering.4–6 In practice, CFRP components are routinely co-assembled with dissimilar materials, where reliable adhesion is hindered by the intrinsically low surface energy of the resin matrix, hydrophobic surfaces, and residual mold-release agents.7 Conventional pretreatments—such as solvent cleaning, chemical etching, and mechanical abrasion—can improve bonding but are constrained by low throughput, environmental burdens, operator dependence, and the risk of fiber damage and nonuniform surface morphology.8,9 Against this backdrop, laser-based surface treatments have emerged as attractive alternatives, offering non-contact, maskless processing, precise energy delivery, and Journal of Applied Physics ARTICLE pubs.aip.org/aip/jap J. [PAGE 3] compatibility with complex geometries and automated production lines.10,11 Laser pretreatment improves the adhesion of CFRP by recon- structing the fiber–matrix interface, removing weak resin-rich layers and contaminants, activating surface chemistry, and engi- neering micro-/nanoscale textures that increase roughness and wet- tability. Zuo et al.12 demonstrated that femtosecond laser processing can selectively remove the polymer matrix while pre- serving fiber integrity and fabricate hierarchical micro-/nanostruc- tures that enhance hydrophilicity (contact angle ≈28.1°). Building on selective ablation, Yao et al.13 employed a two-color double- pulse femtosecond scheme to achieve resin-targeted removal without fiber damage, thereby improving surface roughness and wettability. To further augment interfacial activation, Yuan et al.14 combined laser texturing with chemical–ultrasonic assistance, increasing surface free energy (∼74.8 mJ/m2) and the O/C ratio (∼33.3%), and delivering ∼48% gains in tensile shear strength with ∼226× areal throughput. From a processing-efficiency standpoint, Li et al.15 used response-surface optimization and defocus strategies to achieve ∼2.25× higher throughput while maintaining mechanical performance, consistent with coupled matrix decomposition and oxidation mechanisms. For unidirectional laminates, Teng et al.16 showed that controlling scan direction and speed (e.g., 45°, 3000 mm/s) enables effective resin removal without fiber damage, improves wettability, and raises tensile shear strength to ∼17.25 MPa with cohesive failure. In terms of photochemical acti- vation, Al-Mahdy et al.17 reported that incoherent UV exposure provides an accessible route, yielding ∼75% improvement over untreated surfaces and ∼10% over NIR-textured ones. Finally, in direct co-bonding configurations without an added adhesive layer, Leone and Genna18 found that laser-treated CFRP surfaces approx- imately double the apparent lap-shear strength. Although laser processing has been widely applied in micro- fabrication and surface-quality control, current processes predomi- nantly rely on Gaussian beams, whose steep fluence gradients readily induce overprocessing at the center and underprocessing at the edges, thereby narrowing the process window and undermining consistency and repeatability. Shaping the beam into a flat-top (top-hat) profile can significantly improve spatial energy unifor- mity, reduce the heat-affected zone (HAZ) and process variability, and such advantages are well documented in prior studies across multiple material systems: achieving high-consistency, low-damage, high-aspect-ratio through-silicon via (TSV) drilling in Si;19 attenu- ating elemental segregation and suppressing Laves-phase formation in Inconel 718 during laser-based deposition/melting,20 and in laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) promoting stable, directional epi- taxial growth with a widened process window21 forming homoge- neous nano-/micro-structures and ultra-smooth surfaces on Ti and GaN;22,23 significantly reducing laser-induced damage (LID) in solar cells;24 and, via vector flat-top-beam shaping based on polari- zation singularities, achieving efficient, controllable material removal and cleaning in comparative process studies.25,26 For polymer–matrix composites, adjustable flat-top rectangular beams have been used for the laser-assisted joining of steel/ carbon-fiber-reinforced thermoplastic (CFRTP) to homogenize the interfacial thermal history and improve joint mechanical performance.27 However, to the best of our knowledge, no systematic study has investigated laser surface treatment or (bioinspired) texturing of CFRP using a flat-top beam. Considering that the resin and carbon fibers in CFRP differ markedly in ablation threshold and thermal properties—differences that amplify the spatial nonuniformity of Gaussian beams, a 355 nm flat-top picosecond laser that simultane- ously textures and activates CFRP surfaces is used to build a bioins- pired “plateau-connected-groove” polygonal pattern derived from tree-frog toe pads, to enhance hydrophilicity. Fluence-dependent morphology, wettability, and chemistry are systematically mapped and correlated with AA7075/CFRP lap-shear performance. The process yields hierarchical micro-/nanotopographies, increases surface polarity without degrading graphitic integrity, and drives a failure-mode transition from interfacial adhesion to fiber-dominated co-failure. Compared with Gaussian-based approaches, the flat-top profile widens the processing window and improves reproducibility. These results provide a high-throughput, automation-ready route for durable multi-material joining of CFRP components. II. EXPERIMENT EQUIPMENT AND DETECTION A. Materials and laser The CFRP used in this study employed an epoxy resin matrix reinforced with SYT49S plain-weave carbon fiber fabric, where both the warp and fill yarns are 12 K carbon fiber yarns, composed of 12 000 individual carbon filaments. The single fiber diameter of the T700 SYT49S is 7 μm, and therefore, the diameter of the 12 K carbon fiber yarn is approximately 0.3– 0.4 mm. The structural adhesive was SW-9, an ambient-curing adhesive. Prior to surface treatment, CFRP specimens were ultrasonically cleaned for 10 min, rinsed with anhydrous alcohol, and air-dried. Single-lap-shear specimens prepared in accordance with GB/T 7124-2008 are shown in Fig. 1(a); each measured 100 × 25 mm with a thickness of 1.6 mm. The bonding schematic is provided in Fig. 1(b). CFRP and AA7075 substrates were bonded with an epoxy adhesive both before and after surface treatment, with a bonding area of 25 × 12.5 mm2. Except for laser texturing applied to the CFRP, the AA7075 surface was sanded to remove oxides and increase rough- ness. Untreated CFRP and aluminum alloy were used as reference samples. A 355 nm picosecond-laser system based on a master oscilla- tor power amplifier configuration was employed for surface micro-/ nanostructuring of CFRP substrates. The laser beam was expanded using a beam expander and subsequently reshaped by a Focal-πShaper_NUV-Q-5 flat-top beam shaper (AdlOptica GmbH, Berlin, Germany; Q-series focal flat-top, NUV, 355 nm) to produce a flat-top irradiance distribution at focus. The shaped beam was guided to a scanning galvanometer via a pair of high-reflectivity 45° mirrors and then focused onto the sample surface through an f-theta lens. The beam trajectory was precisely controlled by the galvanometer system to achieve accurate surface patterning, as illus- trated in Fig. 2 (Table I). B. Considering the laser spot size and processing efficiency, a geomet- rically similar but upscaled pattern was adopted. These dimensions were carefully chosen to balance the trade-off between increasing the contact area and maintaining sufficient drainage capacity. The unit size of 0.45 mm was selected to ensure that each polygonal unit contributes effectively to the increase in the real contact area without overcrowding the surface, thus allowing optimal interaction between the surface and the substrate. Similarly, the groove width of 0.03 mm was selected to create narrow channels that would facil- itate rapid capillary drainage, while still preserving the polygonal cells intact to maintain adhesion performance. Furthermore, the biomimetic polygonal pattern was implemented on CFRP using a 355 nm flat-top picosecond-laser ultraviolet source, as schematized in Fig. 4. C. Characterization method All characterization and measurement procedures were con- ducted using a range of advanced analytical instruments to ensure the comprehensive evaluation of the samples. The surface morphol- ogy and three-dimensional topography were observed using a laser confocal microscope (DCM8, Leica), with an X–Y lateral resolution of no less than 0.14 μm and a Z (height) resolution of up to 0.1 nm. A contact angle goniometer (LSA 100) measured the contact angle to assess surface wettability. An x-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS, ESCALAB Xi+, Thermo Fisher Scientific) ana- lyzed surface chemical composition after laser activation, while a Raman spectrometer (Renishaw inVia Qontor) identified structural changes and chemical composition. Tensile shear strength measurements were conducted using a tensile testing machine (Shimadzu AG-250KNIS). III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION A.
